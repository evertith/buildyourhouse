# Arkansas Owner-Builder Permit Guide

*By a retired general contractor with 15+ years building custom homes — [about the author](/about). Last updated: September 2026.*

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Quick Answer: Can You Build Your Own House in Arkansas?">

**Yes.** Arkansas requires a [residential builders license](https://www.labor.arkansas.gov/licensing/arkansas-contractors-licensing-board/) when the cost of residential work exceeds **$2,000**, but a person building their own residence is exempt under **Ark. Code Ann. § 17-25-513(1)** — the only condition is that you not build more than one residence in any calendar year. There is no holding period, no not-for-sale window, no dollar cap on the exemption, and no state owner-builder affidavit.

The building code is the **Arkansas Fire Prevention Code (AFPC), 2021 Edition**, whose Volume III is the 2021 International Residential Code with Arkansas amendments, effective **January 1, 2023**. Here is the part that trips people up: Arkansas has a statewide *code* and no statewide *permit*. Cities "may" require a building permit; counties have no enumerated power to run a building-code program at all, and most never created one. Of fourteen counties whose own department lists we read in September 2026, exactly one — **Benton** — requires a building permit in unincorporated areas.

Arkansas also has homeowner exemptions for electrical, plumbing and HVAC work, but they are **three differently worded tests**, not one rule, and two of them expressly bow to local ordinance. Read that section before you plan your own trade work.

</CalloutBox>

Arkansas is one of the more contradictory owner-builder states in the country. It writes one building code for the entire state, forbids local governments from adopting a different one — and then requires nobody to enforce it on your house. The code binds your dwelling as a construction standard everywhere. The inspection that would catch a mistake exists only where a city, or one of a handful of counties, decided to create a building department.

That split — one code on the books, an inspector almost nowhere outside city limits — is the single most important thing to understand before you build here. It is genuine freedom for a rural owner-builder and a genuine transfer of responsibility, because the standard still applies to you whether or not anyone shows up to check it.

## Arkansas Building Code Overview

<CalloutBox type="info" title="The Big Picture: one code, and usually no permit">

Arkansas adopts a **single statewide building code** and tells local governments they may not adopt a different one. What it does not do is require anyone to issue a permit, review a plan, or inspect a house.

The code's own text concedes the choice. AFPC Volume I § 101.2.2 says the AFPC "shall be the only foundation document available for modification by local jurisdictions **should they choose to adopt** more stringent provisions." Cities "may" require a permit (Ark. Code Ann. § 14-56-202). Counties have no statute that plainly authorizes a building-code program — they act under a general services power at § 14-14-802 in which "building codes" is not among the enumerated services, and the county planning subchapter enumerates "zoning, subdivision, setback, or entry control" ordinances and again omits building codes (§ 14-17-207(a)).

Nothing is delegated. Every verb is "may."

</CalloutBox>

The AFPC is adopted and enforced by the **State Fire Marshal**, and that office has moved. **Act 841 of 2023** relocated the State Fire Marshal into the **Office of Fire Protection Services, Division of Emergency Management** (Ark. Code Ann. § 20-22-1009), repealed most of the old §§ 12-13-1xx series, and re-enacted the Fire Marshal's duties — including "enforce the Arkansas Fire Prevention Code and periodically revise and update" it — at § 20-22-1010. Almost every guide on the web, including earlier versions of this one, still names the Arkansas State Police, because the 2021 code's own front matter predates the move.

One trap worth naming: **§ 12-13-120 is still on the books and is still titled "Arkansas Fire Prevention Code."** It is a storm-shelter provision for educational occupancies, not the adoption authority. It is exactly the section a search will hand you, and it is the wrong one.

The adopting rule sits in the [Code of Arkansas Rules](https://codeofarrules.arkansas.gov/) under the Office of Fire Protection Services. The code text itself is readable free at [codes.iccsafe.org](https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/ARFPCVIIIR2021P1) — Volume III is the residential one.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Arkansas adopted no IRC appendices — so there is no state tiny-house code path">

AFPC Volume III § R102.5: appendices "are NOT adopted by the State of Arkansas and shall not apply unless adopted by local ordinance," and the volume's table of contents strikes every appendix.

That means Arkansas has adopted **no** Appendix AQ Tiny Houses, no strawbale, cob, light straw-clay or 3D-printed construction appendix, and no private sewage disposal appendix, at state level. If you are planning alternative construction, there is no state code path to point an official at — a local jurisdiction would have to adopt the appendix by ordinance. (A small internal inconsistency to be aware of: the table of contents strikes appendices AA through AX while § R102.5 names AA through AW. Either way, nothing survives.)

</CalloutBox>

**Twelve of fourteen — including three of the four largest counties in the state — require no county building permit in unincorporated areas.** But "no building permit" is never "no permits." Every county in the sample still had at least one construction touchpoint: floodplain, 911 addressing, a road-department driveway tie-in, and the septic permit that applies statewide.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Washington County: no building permit, and the most regulated 'no' in the state">

[Washington County's own Planning page](https://www.washingtoncountyar.gov/government/departments-f-z/planning) states it plainly: the county "does not enforce building codes for agricultural buildings, single-family homes, or residential accessory structures … in unincorporated areas. As a result, no building permits or inspections are required, and no certificates of occupancy will be issued unless specifically requested."

Do not read that as "no rules." The entire county is zoned — every parcel carries "Agricultural/Single-Family Residential, one (1) unit per acre." Planning issues floodplain development permits. County septic ordinances require a permit on a tract of **any** size, with no large-lot exemption. And the county gates your **911 address on an approved soil analysis or perc test**, which inverts the usual order: in Washington County the soil test comes before the address, not after.

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Benton County is the exception — do not let the rural pattern near it">

[Benton County requires building permits](https://bentoncountyar.gov/building-safety/permits/) for new construction, additions and interior remodels of single-family homes in unincorporated areas. Group U accessory structures — detached shops, carports, storage buildings, decks, fences — were exempted from the *building* permit effective November 28, 2023, but **trade permits for electrical, plumbing and HVAC are still required for them**, and Planning's site-plan review still applies even where no building permit does.

If you are lot-shopping across the Benton/Washington county line, that difference is worth more than a few thousand dollars of fees. Confirm it against the county's own page before you buy.

</CalloutBox>

### Arkansas-Specific Amendments and Conditions

The AFPC adapts the base IRC, and local design criteria fill in the site-specific values:

1. **Frost depth**: Shallow — Little Rock's published design criteria set the frost line at **12 inches**, with a minimum footing 18" wide and 12" deep. Northern Arkansas jurisdictions may require deeper footings; verify locally
2. **Wind**: Arkansas sits in the interior severe-storm belt. Little Rock's design criteria use a **115 mph basic (ultimate) design wind speed** for ordinary Risk Category II homes
3. **Seismic**: The northeast counties near the New Madrid Seismic Zone require elevated seismic design (see the dedicated section below); most of the state is low-seismic. Your seismic site class is determined by investigation at your site, not from a citywide map — do not accept a number someone quotes for "Little Rock" as applying to your lot
4. **Snow**: Light — Little Rock design ground snow load is **10 psf**; northern counties somewhat higher
5. **Storm shelters and sprinklers**: Not mandated for one- and two-family dwellings statewide despite heavy tornado activity

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="No statewide sprinkler or storm-shelter mandate">

Arkansas does not require residential fire sprinklers or storm shelters in one- and two-family dwellings, even though the state is squarely in tornado country. Shelters are strongly encouraged but voluntary — see the hazard section for why you should build one anyway.

</CalloutBox>

## Arkansas Owner-Builder Laws

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Where the freedom comes from">

Arkansas licenses residential homebuilders, then carves out a clear statutory exemption for a person building their own residence. That exemption is the legal foundation for owner-building here, and it is unusually clean — one condition, and nothing else.

</CalloutBox>

Arkansas licenses residential builders through the [Arkansas Contractors Licensing Board](https://www.labor.arkansas.gov/licensing/arkansas-contractors-licensing-board/), part of the Department of Labor and Licensing. Title 17, Chapter 25 actually holds **two separate regimes**, and confusing them is the fastest way to publish a wrong number:

- **Subchapters 1–3 are commercial.** The threshold there is $50,000 including labor and materials — and § 17-25-101(a)(1) expressly excepts "single-family residences," with § 17-25-101(c) restating that the definition covers everything "excepting only single-family residences." **The $50,000 figure never applies to a house.**
- **Subchapter 5 is residential**, administered by the Residential Contractors Committee. The threshold there is **$2,000** (§ 17-25-513(3)), with an anti-splitting rule so you cannot carve a job into sub-$2,000 contracts.

The statutes are published in full by the Board itself: [Residential Contractors Licensing Law](https://labor.arkansas.gov/wp-content/uploads/Residential-Contractor-Law-revised-04.2026.pdf) and [Commercial Contractors Licensing Law](https://labor.arkansas.gov/wp-content/uploads/Commercial-Contractors-Law-revised-04.2026.pdf), both revised April 2026.

### Legal Rights

Under **Ark. Code Ann. § 17-25-513**:

- **(1)** A person "who acts as a residential building contractor in the construction of his or her residence" is exempt, "unless he or she builds more than one (1) residence during any calendar year"
- **(2)** The owner of a single-family residence acting as their own home-improvement contractor on their own property is exempt — with no annual cap in its text
- **(3)** Any project where the cost does not exceed $2,000 is exempt, with no contract splitting

Two supporting definitions do a lot of work here. **"Own residence" is defined by rule** at 17 CAR § 295-101(6) to mean "the personal residence, the principal place of abode, the domicile, **or a residence constructed for the occupancy of the person who owns the property**." That final clause is what makes the exemption work for a house you do not live in yet — without it, "principal place of abode" would be circular for new construction.

And the exemption reaches **managing** the job, not just swinging the hammer. "Residential building contractor" is defined at § 17-25-502(2) to include one who "assumes charge in a supervisory capacity or otherwise manages the construction" of a single-family residence. Acting as your own general contractor and hiring subs is inside the exempt activity.

One more definition worth knowing: § 17-25-502(3) defines "single family residence" as a project of "at least one (1) but no more than four (4) units of new construction." A duplex, triplex or fourplex is still residential in Arkansas, not commercial.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="The one-home-per-year line is a real statute, not a norm">

In most states the "one home per year" limit is an informal local policy. In Arkansas it is written into the licensing exemption itself: build **more than one residence in a calendar year** and you lose the owner-builder exemption and need a residential builders license. Build one home for yourself and you are clear.

What the statute does not tell you is how the count is measured — start of construction, completion, or certificate of occupancy. A build that straddles a year boundary is ambiguous on the text. If that is your situation, ask the Residential Contractors Committee in writing before you start the second one.

</CalloutBox>

### What the Statute Does Not Say

This matters as much as what it does say, because most of the conditions people expect Arkansas to impose are imported from other states' rules. Reading § 17-25-513 and the whole of 17 CAR part 295:

- **No holding period.** Nothing requires you to keep the house for any length of time
- **No sale-timing restriction and no resale clawback.** There is no "not for sale or lease" clause anywhere in the exemption. Arkansas is genuinely unlike the states that impose a one- or two-year no-sale rule
- **No owner-builder affidavit and no state exemption form.** The exemption is self-executing — there is nothing to file with the Board, and its forms index carries no owner-builder form. A city that issues permits may still want proof of ownership at the counter; that is a local document, not a state one
- **No dollar limit on the owner-builder exemption.** The $2,000 figure is a *different* exemption, § 17-25-513(3), for small jobs
- **No occupancy period.** The rule's test is purpose at the time of building — "constructed for the occupancy of the person who owns the property" — not a minimum number of months in residence

The things that do still apply: local permits and inspections where a jurisdiction issues them, and standard Arkansas residential property disclosure obligations when you later sell.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Hiring an unlicensed contractor is a penalty on YOU, not just on them">

This is the least-known provision in the Arkansas licensing law and it points straight at the owner. **Ark. Code Ann. § 17-25-510(b)** authorizes a civil penalty of "not less than one hundred dollars ($100) nor more than four hundred dollars ($400) for each day that the violation occurred" — and subdivision **(b)(2)** applies that penalty to **"using a contractor in violation of this chapter."**

Hiring an unlicensed sub is not only the sub's problem. Verify every license at the Board's [contractor lookup](https://www.labor.arkansas.gov/licensing/arkansas-contractors-licensing-board/find-a-licensed-contractor/) before the first invoice, and keep the printout in your job file.

The flip side protects you: under § 17-25-515 a residential contractor who works in violation of the subchapter "shall not bring an action" to enforce the contract **or for quantum meruit**. Unlike the commercial chapter, there is no cure provision. An unlicensed residential contractor in Arkansas cannot sue you for the money.

</CalloutBox>

### Homeowner Doing Their Own Trade Work

Every guide you will read says Arkansas lets you do your own electrical, plumbing and HVAC. All three exemptions do exist. But read the verbs — **they are three different tests**, and the differences decide whether they reach a house that is not finished and not occupied.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="The honest answer on new construction: nobody has resolved it">

Whether these three exemptions reach an **unoccupied new build** is the highest-value unresolved question in Arkansas owner-builder law. There is no Arkansas appellate case on it, no board rule, and no published opinion letter settling it. Anyone who tells you flatly that you may wire and plumb your own new house in Arkansas is reading past the words.

The HVACR one is the starkest — "existing building or structure" plainly describes something that already exists. On the text alone, an owner-builder installing a system in a house they are building is not inside it.

What to do about it: write to each board — the Board of Electrical Examiners, the HVACR Licensing Board (both at the Department of Labor and Licensing) and the [Plumbing and Natural Gas Section](https://healthy.arkansas.gov/programs-services/licensing-military-member-licensure-permits-plan-reviews/plumbing-natural-gas-health-code/) at the Department of Health — describe your actual situation, and keep the written answers in the job file. That is the only way to be certain, and a written answer is worth having if anyone questions the work later.

</CalloutBox>

Two further constraints on the electrical side that most guides miss:

- **A city or county may require you to prove competency first.** Ark. Code Ann. § 17-28-305(b)(2): a city or county "may by ordinance require a person, before doing electrical work on his or her primary residence, to demonstrate a technical competency to comply with the city or county standards." Ask your jurisdiction whether it has done so
- **Even an exempt homeowner still needs local permits where they exist.** § 17-33-102(d) says it outright for HVACR: a person exempt under that section "is required to conform to rules on the performance of HVACR work as well as obtaining local permits and inspections as may be required by local ordinance"

And one on the plumbing side worth raising with the Department of Health if you build inside a city or a water, sewer or gas utility district: **§ 17-38-301(b)(1)** separately requires that in any such area, "no person, firm, or corporation shall install plumbing unless a licensed master or restricted licensed plumber who shall be responsible for proper installation is in charge at all times." That is a jobsite supervision mandate, textually distinct from the licensing exemption, and how the two fit together for an owner-builder is not settled in the statute.

### Liability and Insurance

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="As owner-builder, the liability is yours">

As an owner-builder in Arkansas:

- You're personally liable for injuries on your job site (carry workers' comp for any paid labor)
- Builder's risk insurance is available but priced higher than for licensed builders
- Some construction lenders require owner-builders to carry liability coverage during the build
- Arkansas residential property disclosure obligations apply for years after you sell
- The electrical homeowner exemption carries an express sting in its own text: it "shall not be referred to in any way and shall not be any evidence of the lack of negligence or the exercise of due care" in a civil damages trial (§ 17-28-102(b))

</CalloutBox>

## Energy Code Requirements

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="We are not printing an Arkansas insulation table, and here is why">

AFPC Volume III deletes IRC Chapter 11 in its entirety and points to the separate **Arkansas Energy Code for New Building Construction**. That much is verified from the code's own table of contents.

What we could **not** verify in September 2026 is the current edition or the instrument that adopts it. The widely repeated answer is a weakened 2009 IECC base with 2014 amendments, but the pages that carried it sit on retired agency paths and no current adopting document or agency landing page could be reached. The Energy Office sits within the **Department of Energy and Environment** — not, as a great many guides still say, the Arkansas Economic Development Commission.

We would rather tell you the honest state of the record than print an R-value table you might build to. **Ask your jurisdiction, or the Energy Office, which edition applies, and get the answer in writing before you order insulation and windows.**

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="The asymmetry nobody tells you: plumbing inspection is mandatory, electrical is optional">

These two read as a matched pair and they are not. The difference is one verb.

**Plumbing — "shall."** The Arkansas plumbing code is adopted as "minimum standards **statewide in application** … to all types of buildings, private or public, **rural or urban**" (§ 17-38-103). And § 17-38-204(c) requires that a city, town, sewerage district, water district, water association, utility gas system or county having a water, sewer or gas utility system "**shall establish a system of permits and inspections**." Work may not be started "without the prescribed licenses, permits, and acceptable plan review" where required (§ 17-38-204(b)(2)). Where a utility system exists but no local inspector was provided, the Department of Health "may take immediate charge and entire control of the plumbing inspection program" (§ 17-38-204(f)(1)).

**Electrical — "may."** § 17-28-305(c): "Any city or county **may** establish by ordinance, rules, and regulations a system of permits and inspections for the installation, repair, and maintenance of electrical facilities and electrical work."

So in a rural Arkansas build your electrical work may genuinely never be inspected — while the plumbing, if you are inside any kind of utility district, is supposed to be.

**But the state kept a reach-in on electrical.** Under 17 CAR § 210-1101(a) a state electrical inspector "may require electrical work to be exposed for inspection, **including the removal of sheetrock**" where the work was never subject to city inspection and there is evidence of serious violations. Exempt from routine inspection is not the same as beyond reach.

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Arkansas is on the 2026 NEC — with its own amendments">

This is the opposite of the usual trap. Most states lag on the electrical code; Arkansas is current. **17 CAR § 210-401** adopts the **National Electrical Code, 2026 edition**, as "the standard for the construction, installation, repair, and maintenance of electrical facilities and the performance of electrical work" — published in the [Board of Electrical Examiners' own administrative rules](https://labor.arkansas.gov/wp-content/uploads/17CARpt-210-Final.pdf).

It does not adopt it clean, and the amendments land in your kitchen and laundry:

- AFCI and GFCI protection is **not required on dwelling-unit circuits over 130 volts**, except for Articles 680 and 682 (§ 210-401(b)(2))
- AFCI/GFCI **does not apply** within a dwelling unit to laundry areas — except convenience outlets within **six feet** of a sink — or to a refrigerator or microwave appliance (§ 210-401(b)(3))
- Arkansas writes its own **island and peninsula receptacle** spacing: at least one within the island or peninsula for the first 9 sq ft of countertop, one more per additional 18 sq ft, and at least one within two feet of the outer end of a peninsula (§ 210-401(b)(4))
- The rule also adopts **ANSI/NECA 1-2023** as the workmanship standard, and § 210-401(f) says the adoption "does not include any later amendments or editions" — a newer NEC does not take effect here until the Board adopts it

Confirm the edition with the Board before you buy the code book, since the rule text itself carries no effective date.

</CalloutBox>

## New Madrid Seismic and Tornado / High-Wind Hazards

This is the section that makes Arkansas different from almost every other Southern state. Two natural hazards drive real design decisions here: violent spring tornadoes statewide, and a serious earthquake threat in the northeast from the New Madrid Seismic Zone.

### New Madrid Seismic Zone (Northeast Arkansas)

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Northeast Arkansas is genuine earthquake country">

The [New Madrid Seismic Zone runs through northeast Arkansas](https://geology.arkansas.gov/maps-and-data/geohazard_maps/GH-EQ-NMSZ-005-new-madrid-seismic-zone-of-northeast-arkansas.html) — roughly northeast from Marked Tree (Poinsett County) up through Blytheville (Mississippi County). This is the most seismically hazardous region in the central United States. Under Arkansas's seismic-zone statute, **Ark. Code Ann. § 12-80-103**, counties including **Craighead and Mississippi** sit in the zone of greatest anticipated damage.

</CalloutBox>

If you build in the northeast — Mississippi, Craighead, Poinsett, Greene, Clay, Crittenden, and neighboring counties — seismic design is not optional theater:

- The site soils (deep, soft Mississippi Embayment sediments) **amplify ground motion**, which is why this region's seismic design loads are so high relative to the rest of the state
- Your foundation, wall bracing, and connections should be detailed for the seismic design category that applies to **your site**, established by investigation rather than assumed from a regional map — a structural engineer is strongly recommended in NE Arkansas
- The 1811–1812 New Madrid earthquakes were among the largest in recorded U.S. history; a major event remains a credible long-term risk
- Note that the statewide seismic *statute* (§ 12-80-103) is written for public structures; for your house, the binding design requirements come from the AFPC/IRC seismic provisions for your site — but the hazard is the same, so build to it

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="In the northeast, pay for the engineer">

Even where an unincorporated county will not inspect your home, the New Madrid hazard is real and physics does not care about permits. Budget for a geotechnical evaluation and an engineered foundation and lateral design if you build anywhere near the NE Arkansas seismic zone. It is the single highest-value professional dollar you will spend there.

</CalloutBox>

### Tornadoes and High Wind (Statewide)

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Build the safe room — the state won't make you, but you'll want it">

Arkansas is hit by violent tornadoes most springs. The code does **not** require a storm shelter in a one- or two-family home, and [Arkansas's homeowner shelter rebate program ended in 2016](https://dps.arkansas.gov/emergency-management/adem/grants-funding/hazard-mitigation-grants/). There is no state cash to help you build one anymore — but it is still the best safety money you can spend.

</CalloutBox>

Wind and shelter considerations:

- **Design wind speed**: Little Rock's design criteria use a **115 mph basic (ultimate) design wind speed** for ordinary homes — design your roof-to-wall and wall-to-foundation connections accordingly
- **Shelters**: Consider a FEMA P-361 above-ground safe room or a reinforced below-grade shelter; a hardened interior closet is a common owner-built approach
- **Cost**: $4,000–$10,000 for a basic in-home safe room (illustrative)
- **Community shelters**: Arkansas has funded [community safe rooms](https://dps.arkansas.gov/emergency-management/adem/plan-prepare/community-safe-rooms/) through FEMA and ADEM grant programs — useful context, but not a substitute for shelter at home

## Special Arkansas Considerations

### Expansive Clay Soils (The Real Foundation Risk)

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Expansive clay is the number one foundation hazard in Arkansas">

Arkansas's biggest foundation problem is not frost — it is [expansive clay](https://geology.arkansas.gov/geohazards/expansive-soils.html). In the Delta, **Sharkey clay** ("buckshot") swells when wet and shrinks when dry, putting enormous stress on slabs and footings. Get a geotechnical evaluation before you pour.

</CalloutBox>

Where it matters most and what to do:

- **Delta / eastern Arkansas**: Sharkey and related clays formed from Mississippi River slackwater deposits; severe shrink-swell
- **Parts of central Arkansas and Little Rock**: Clay-heavy soils that move with wet/dry and freeze/thaw cycles
- Mitigation: geotechnical evaluation, properly compacted and stable base, footings on undisturbed soil, careful site drainage to keep perimeter moisture stable, and on bad sites engineered slabs or pier-and-beam systems
- Damage modes are "edge lift" and "center lift" — both crack slabs and rack framing if the foundation is not designed for the soil

### Septic Systems (Rural Areas)

This is the permit that applies to you **whether or not your county has a building department**, and it is the one that most often determines whether a rural parcel is buildable at all. The Arkansas Department of Health regulates onsite wastewater under Ark. Code Ann. § 14-236-101 et seq. and the **Rules Pertaining to Onsite Wastewater Systems**, effective September 5, 2024.

**The sequence, and where the surprises are:**

1. **The approval comes before the house.** Rule § 4.3 requires the application and plans to be submitted to and approved by the Department or its Authorized Agent "**prior to construction of a building or residence**." Not before occupancy — before construction
2. **You cannot design it yourself.** Rule § 4.10.1: Part I of the permit application "**shall be completed by a Designated Representative**" — a private licensed professional (engineer, surveyor, master plumber, registered sanitarian or similar) certified by the Department to make percolation tests, designs and inspections. Your county health unit will give you the list of DRs working in your area
3. **You may install it yourself.** Ark. Code Ann. § 14-236-102(b)(2) requires registration of installers "**with the individual homeowner retaining all rights to install and repair his system**," and "installer" is defined as someone who does the work for compensation **for others** (§ 14-236-103(6)). So a homeowner may lawfully install their own system — but the permit is still required, the DR must still design it, and field practice varies by county Environmental Health Specialist. Confirm with your county health unit before you rent the equipment
4. **Soil first, perc test second.** Arkansas is a soil-morphology state: a **minimum of two soil pits** is required, one in the primary absorption area and one in the secondary (§ 7.1), left open for the Authorized Agent. A percolation test is an option after that, not the starting point (§ 8.5)
5. **You need two absorption areas, not one.** Every design requires both a **primary and a secondary (reserve)** absorption area. Buyers shopping raw land routinely underestimate this — plan on roughly twice the suitable soil area you were expecting
6. **The system may not be used until the Permit for Operation issues** (§ 4.10.3), which comes after the installation inspection

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="The separation distances that decide your site plan">

Rule § 6.2 holds the **entire** statewide separation table for onsite wastewater — and Arkansas deliberately gives the septic tank and the absorption field the **same** numbers, so one set covers tank, field and lines:

- **100 feet** from any source of domestic water supply — this is the well number
- **100 feet** from the high-water mark of any stream or lake, and **100 feet** from any sinkhole
- **10 feet** from any dwelling or building, **10 feet** from all property lines, **10 feet** from any water service line
- **50 feet** from a pond on the same property; **100 feet** from a pond on adjacent property if in the pond watershed
- **300 feet** from a spring used as a public water supply, or from the high-water mark of a body of water within a quarter mile of a public water supply intake

Two things to hold onto. First, these are **floors, not entitlements**: § 6.1 says minimum distances apply "only where ideal conditions indicate them to be sufficient and greater distances shall be required where local conditions demand." Second, the well side of the table has a multiplier almost nobody publishes — 17 CAR § 11-502(c)(2) requires the lateral distances to be **increased, "double the distance for highly pervious gravel formations."** In Ozark karst that quietly turns 100 feet into 200.

Sketch the layout before the evaluator walks the lot — the [Site Plan Studio](/site-plan-studio) is a free tool that places the well, tank and absorption field to scale, measures the separation between each, and prints the sheet at letter size for your permit packet.

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="The 10-acre septic exemption — real, current, and missing from the rule book">

**Ark. Code Ann. § 14-236-104(c):** "The requirements of this chapter shall not apply to any individual sewage disposal system or alternate and experimental system which is situated on a tract of land **ten (10) acres or larger**, in which the field line or sewage disposal line is no closer than **two hundred feet (200′) to the property line**."

The Department's own [onsite wastewater guidance](https://healthy.arkansas.gov/programs-services/public-health-safety/onsite-wastewater/onsite-wastewater-faqs/) confirms and sharpens it: it applies to a **single residence** on ten or more acres where all parts of the system are more than 200 feet from any property line, "**including roads**" — and the owner is still required to install and operate the system so it meets state requirements and does not create a nuisance.

**Why almost nobody finds it:** the exemption is **absent from the 81-page 2024 rule**. A full-text search of the rule for "ten (10) acres," "10 acres" and "two hundred feet" turns up nothing relevant; the rule's variances-and-exemptions section mirrors § 14-236-104(a) and (b) and omits (c). Cite the statute, not the rule, and expect county health staff to be unfamiliar with it.

**Run the geometry before you count on it.** On a square 10-acre parcel (about 660 feet a side), a 200-foot buffer on every side leaves roughly a 260-by-260-foot envelope — tight but workable. On a long narrow 10-acre parcel it can be geometrically impossible.

**And think hard before using it.** § 14-236-104(b) still lets the Department act on a system that becomes a health hazard or nuisance, a municipality may impose stricter local rules (§ 14-236-105), and an unpermitted system can create real problems at appraisal, resale and mortgage underwriting. On most parcels the $30 to $150 plan-review fee is cheap insurance against a financing problem five years out.

</CalloutBox>

### Wells

Private wells are common in rural Arkansas, and the regulator is not who most guides say it is. Water well construction is governed by the **Arkansas Water Well Construction Act**, Ark. Code Ann. § 17-50-101 et seq., and the **Arkansas Water Well Construction Rules** at 17 CAR Part 11. The Department of Health does not regulate well construction.

**The agency changed in 2023.** Act 691 of 2023 **abolished the Commission on Water Well Construction** and transferred its authority, duties, records, personnel and appropriations to the **Arkansas Natural Resources Commission**, on the Natural Resources side of the Department of Agriculture. Existing licenses and rules were preserved. Do not go looking for the old commission — it does not exist.

**Three facts that matter to an owner-builder:**

- **You may drill your own well.** § 17-50-108(b): nothing in the chapter "shall prevent a person who has not obtained a license … from constructing, altering, or repairing a water well or installing or repairing a pump or pumping equipment **for use by him or her on his or her own land**"
- **The exemption is from licensing, not from the standards.** § 17-50-107(a) still bars anyone from constructing, repairing or abandoning a well "without complying with the provisions of this chapter and the rules." Casing, grouting, well seals, disinfection and the separation distances all still apply to you
- **There is no well permit and no notice of intent** for a private domestic well. The only permit in the rules is a contractor-side rig permit. What does exist is a **report of construction**, due to the Commission **within 90 days** and required to carry the well's longitude and latitude (17 CAR § 11-401); a copy goes to the customer on demand (§ 11-403)

Well siting rules worth knowing before you pick the spot: 100 feet from a septic tank, absorption field, cess pool, leaching pit, pit privy or manure pile; 50 feet from watertight sewers; **doubled in highly pervious gravel formations** (§ 11-502(c)); at least 10 feet clear of any building projection and 10 feet from any power line, and the casing top may **not** terminate in a basement or an undrained below-grade pit (§ 11-502(h)). Closed-loop geothermal wells keep 50 feet from a septic tank, its field lines, or other potable wells (§ 11-502(e)).

### Flooding (Delta and River Corridors)

Eastern Arkansas and the major river bottoms (Mississippi, Arkansas, White, Red) carry real floodplain risk. Check FEMA flood maps before buying; building in a Special Flood Hazard Area triggers elevation requirements and flood insurance.

This is also the permit most likely to exist in a county that has no building department at all. Faulkner County requires a floodplain permit countywide before any development; Garland County issues floodplain construction permits through Emergency Management, and an exemption certificate if you are outside the hazard area; Benton and Washington counties both administer floodplain permits through their planning offices. Ask about it early — it is easy to assume "no building department" means "no county paperwork," and that is wrong nearly everywhere.

## Top Counties for Owner-Builders

### 1. **Washington County** (Fayetteville, Springdale — NW Arkansas)
- **Pros**: University town, strong economy, and — confirmed in the county's own words — no building permits, inspections or certificates of occupancy for single-family homes in unincorporated areas
- **Cons**: The most regulated "no" in the state — countywide zoning at one unit per acre, floodplain permits, a septic permit required on any size tract, and a perc test before you get a 911 address. City builds in Fayetteville carry full fees; land costs are high near town
- **Best for**: Owner-builders who want NW Arkansas growth with a rural unincorporated build option, and who read the zoning ordinance first

### 2. **Benton County** (Bentonville, Rogers — NW Arkansas)
- **Pros**: Strongest economy and job growth in the state, excellent resale; Group U accessory structures exempt from the building permit in unincorporated areas
- **Cons**: The one county in our sample that **does** require a building permit outside city limits; trade permits still required on exempt accessory structures; city impact fees around $3,800 in Bentonville; rising land prices; 4A climate adds insulation cost
- **Best for**: Owner-builders who want appreciation and amenities and can absorb the NW Arkansas land and fee costs

### 3. **Faulkner County** (Conway — central Arkansas)
- **Pros**: Fast-growing Little Rock satellite, no county building department, reasonable city fees, good access to the metro
- **Cons**: A floodplain permit is required countywide before any development; some expansive-soil areas; verify enforcement inside Conway, Mayflower, Wooster, Vilonia and Greenbrier
- **Best for**: Central-Arkansas owner-builders wanting metro access at lower cost than Little Rock proper

### 4. **Saline County** (Benton, Bryant — Little Rock suburbs)
- **Pros**: Popular Little Rock bedroom communities, solid schools, no county building department (subdivision review only)
- **Cons**: Growth pressure on land prices
- **Best for**: Little Rock-area owner-builders wanting suburban value

### 5. **Ozark counties** (Baxter, Boone, Marion, Carroll, Stone, Searcy)
- **Pros**: Low land cost, scenic, and no county building department in the ones we were able to verify (Baxter and Boone both publish department lists with no building or planning office)
- **Cons**: 4A climate, limited employment, financing can be harder without permits or a CO — and karst geology means the gravel-formation doubling on well separations may apply to your site. Several of these counties publish no enumerable department list, so verify by phone rather than assuming
- **Best for**: Owner-builders prioritizing rural lifestyle, low cost, and minimal red tape

### Most Expensive / Challenging Areas

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="These areas mean stricter rules, higher costs, or tougher sites">

The jurisdictions below carry the highest fees, strictest review, or toughest site conditions in the state — go in with eyes open.

</CalloutBox>

- **City of Bentonville**: Highest impact fees in NW Arkansas (roughly $3,800 per home) plus design standards

## Common Questions

**Do I need a license to build my own house in Arkansas?** No. Arkansas requires a residential builders license when residential work exceeds $2,000, but a person building their own residence is exempt under Ark. Code Ann. § 17-25-513(1) — as long as you do not build more than one residence in a calendar year. That is the only condition: no holding period, no resale restriction, no affidavit. You still pull local building permits in jurisdictions that require them.

**Can you build your own house without a permit in Arkansas?** In much of the state, yes — because there is no permit to pull. Arkansas has a statewide building code but no statewide building permit and no state residential inspection program. Cities "may" require one and most do; counties have no enumerated power to run a building-code program, and of fourteen counties we checked against their own department lists in September 2026, only Benton required a building permit in unincorporated areas. Confirm your own county before you assume it either way.

**What is the Arkansas owner-builder exemption?** It is a real statute. § 17-25-513(1) exempts a person acting as a residential building contractor in the construction of his or her residence, unless more than one residence is built in a calendar year. "Own residence" is defined by rule at 17 CAR § 295-101(6) to include "a residence constructed for the occupancy of the person who owns the property," which is what makes it work for a house you do not live in yet. There is no not-for-sale window and no affidavit.

**Can a homeowner do their own electrical, plumbing, and HVAC work in Arkansas?** All three exemptions exist, but they are three different tests. Electrical covers "his or her primary residence," defined as a dwelling "used as" your primary place of residence. Plumbing covers a building "owned and occupied by him or her as his or her home." HVACR reaches work "in an existing building or structure" — which on its face is not a house under construction. Electrical and plumbing are both expressly subject to local ordinance, and a city may require you to demonstrate technical competency before doing your own electrical work. Ask each board in writing about your specific situation.

**Can I install my own septic system in Arkansas?** Yes, you may install it — § 14-236-102(b)(2) preserves "the individual homeowner retaining all rights to install and repair his system." But you may not design it: Part I of the application "shall be completed by a Designated Representative" (Onsite Wastewater Rules § 4.10.1). The permit is required either way, the approval must come before construction of the residence, and the system may not be used until the Permit for Operation issues.

**Do I need a permit to drill a well in Arkansas?** No — there is no well permit and no notice of intent for a private domestic well, and § 17-50-108(b) expressly lets a landowner construct a well for their own use on their own land without a license. What you do owe is compliance with every construction standard in 17 CAR Part 11, and a report of construction within 90 days carrying the well's longitude and latitude.

**How much does an Arkansas owner-builder permit cost?** In cities, building permits for a typical 2,000 sq ft home run roughly $900–$1,550 on a valuation basis, plus plan review and trade permits. Northwest Arkansas adds impact fees — Bentonville's run around $3,800 per home on the schedule we found, which dates from 2023. In a county with no building department the building-permit cost is $0, but the septic plan review ($30 to $150) and often a floodplain permit still apply.

**Which Arkansas counties are best for owner-builders?** Washington County pairs NW Arkansas growth with no building permit in unincorporated areas, at the cost of countywide zoning and a septic ordinance with no acreage exemption. Benton County has the strongest economy but is the one county in our sample that requires a building permit outside city limits. Faulkner and Saline suit central-Arkansas builders. The Ozark counties offer the lowest cost and least red tape, with harder financing and karst geology as the trade-off.

**Does Arkansas require a storm shelter?** No. Despite heavy tornado activity, the code does not require a storm shelter in one- and two-family homes, and the state homeowner rebate program ended in 2016. Most experienced Arkansas owner-builders build one anyway.

## Final Thoughts for Arkansas Owner-Builders

Arkansas rewards owner-builders who match their build to the state's split personality. Inside the cities — Little Rock, Fayetteville, Bentonville — you get a real code, real inspections, and reasonable fees (impact fees aside). Out in the unincorporated counties, you will often face no building permit at all, which is genuine freedom and genuine responsibility in equal measure.

The big decisions:

1. **Establish your enforcement level deliberately, in writing.** Pull your county's own department list and see whether a building office exists. A no-permit county means freedom but also no safety net and tougher financing and resale. A city means oversight but cleaner title and easier loans. Choose on purpose
2. **Use the owner-builder exemption correctly**: one home per calendar year, built for your own occupancy. Stay inside § 17-25-513(1) and you need no builders license — and do not import conditions from other states that Arkansas never wrote
3. **Verify every contractor's license before the first invoice.** § 17-25-510(b)(2) puts a $100-to-$400-per-day civil penalty on the owner who uses a contractor in violation of the chapter. That one is on you, not on them
4. **Get the trade exemptions in writing before you plan on them.** Three different tests, and the HVACR one says "existing building." Ask each board about your actual situation rather than trusting a summary — including this one
5. **Treat the septic permit as the real gate.** It applies whether or not your county has a building department, the approval must come before you build, and you cannot design the system yourself. If your parcel is ten acres or more, read § 14-236-104(c) carefully before deciding whether to use it
6. **Engineer for the soil and the seismic zone.** Expansive clay statewide and New Madrid seismic in the northeast are the two things that wreck Arkansas houses. Spend on geotech and structural engineering even where no one makes you
7. **Build the storm shelter.** The state will not require it or pay for it anymore. Build it anyway

Arkansas is a strong state for the practical, self-reliant owner-builder. Pick the right county, respect the soil and the seismic zone, get the trade questions answered in writing, and the freedom here is real.
