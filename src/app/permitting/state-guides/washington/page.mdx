# Washington State Owner-Builder Permit Guide

*By a retired general contractor with 15+ years building custom homes — [about the author](/about). Last updated: August 2026.*

Washington State offers owner-builders strong legal protections combined with strict building codes driven by seismic requirements and energy efficiency goals. From the wet westside to the dry eastside, the state presents unique challenges and opportunities for those building their own homes.

The thing to understand about Washington is that it regulates construction through **contractor registration**, not a general contractor "license." Anyone doing construction for others must register with the Department of Labor & Industries (L&I). As an owner working on **your own property**, you're exempt from that registration under [RCW 18.27.090(12)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.27.090) — but the two trades Washington licenses strictly statewide, **electrical and plumbing**, have their own homeowner rules you have to follow.

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Quick Answer: Can You Build Your Own House in Washington?">

**Yes.** Washington has no general contractor "license" — it has **contractor registration** through L&I, and a property owner working on their **own** property is exempt from that registration under [RCW 18.27.090(12)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.27.090). You can pull permits and act as your own general contractor on a home you own. The catch: that exemption is lost if you do the work for the purpose of **selling, demolishing, or leasing** the property, and a separate provision ([RCW 18.27.090(11)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.27.090)) creates a problem if you sell or lease improved property you've owned for **less than twelve months**. Electrical and plumbing are licensed statewide — but Washington specifically lets a property owner do their **own** electrical and plumbing on property they own. [RCW 19.28.261(1)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.28.261) exempts an owner doing electrical work "at his or her residence... or on other property owned by him or her" with **no occupancy-duration condition**; the widely-repeated 24-month affidavit belongs to the *next* sentence of the statute, which covers a new residential building of up to four units **intended for rent, sale, or lease**. You still need a **Property Owner Electrical Work Permit** from L&I plus inspection. Anyone you **hire** for electrical or plumbing must be licensed.

</CalloutBox>

### Key Washington Amendments

1. **Seismic Requirements**: Extensive (most of state SDC D or higher)
2. **Energy Code**: Among strictest in nation (only California comparable)
3. **Ventilation**: Enhanced requirements for moisture control
4. **Wildfire Protection**: Growing requirements in eastern WA and foothills
5. **Landslide/Steep Slope**: Special requirements in many areas

## Owner-Builder Laws (RCW 18.27.090)

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Where the freedom comes from">

Washington does not issue a general contractor "license." Instead, anyone who does construction work for others must **register as a contractor** with L&I. The owner-builder path is an **exemption** from that registration.

</CalloutBox>

Anyone who does construction work for others must register as a contractor with L&I under [Chapter 18.27 RCW](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.27).

### Legal Rights

Under [RCW 18.27.090(12)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.27.090), a property owner may:
- Build a single-family residence on property they own
- Pull permits as an owner-builder without registering as a contractor
- Perform the work themselves
- Act as their own general contractor

The subsection is usually quoted only halfway. The full exemption reads: "Any person working on his or her own property, whether occupied by him or her or not, and any person working on his or her personal residence, whether owned by him or her or not." That second clause matters and rarely gets mentioned: it covers work on the home you actually live in even when the deed isn't in your name.

**Hiring subcontractors is a different subsection.** When you contract with a registered contractor to do part of the work, you're relying on [RCW 18.27.090(11)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.27.090) — the exemption for an owner who contracts with a **registered** contractor — not (12). Two consequences: the subs you hire must actually be registered with L&I for the exemption to hold, and (11) carries its own carve-out for leasing or selling improved property you've owned less than twelve months (below).

### Critical Restrictions

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Build to live in, not to flip">

**Not for sale, demolition, or lease**: The owner-property exemption in [RCW 18.27.090(12)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.27.090) "shall not apply to any person who performs the activities of a contractor on his or her own property for the **purpose of selling, demolishing, or leasing** the property." If you build to flip or rent, you generally need to be registered.

**The 12-month rule**: A separate provision, [RCW 18.27.090(11)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.27.090), removes the related exemption for someone who acts as a contractor "for the purpose of leasing or selling improved property he or she has owned for **less than twelve months**." Practically, selling or leasing soon after completion can put you on the wrong side of the registration law — talk to a real estate attorney before you start if there's any chance you'll sell early.

</CalloutBox>

**Owner-occupancy**: There is no statewide owner-occupancy mandate written into RCW 18.27.090 for the property-owner exemption — the text says "whether occupied by him or her or not." Occupancy only becomes a condition in the narrow rent/sale/lease branch of the electrical statute described below. Many local permit offices do ask you to sign an owner-builder/owner-as-contractor affidavit; verify your jurisdiction's affidavit requirements locally.

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="Falsifying an exemption forfeits your permit">

Under [RCW 18.27.110](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.27.110), a building permit obtained by falsifying information to claim an exemption is forfeited.

</CalloutBox>

### Licensed Trades — and the property-owner exceptions

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="You can do your own electrical and plumbing on property you own">

Washington licenses **electricians and plumbers** strictly through L&I. But the law specifically lets a **property owner do their own** electrical and plumbing work. Both exemptions are broader than most guides claim — and the plumbing one is broader than the electrical one.

</CalloutBox>

The conditions and permits differ by trade:

- **Electrical (property you own)**: [RCW 19.28.261(1)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.28.261) exempts a property owner doing electrical work "at his or her residence... or on other property owned by him or her." There is **no occupancy-duration condition** in that sentence. You must buy a **Property Owner Electrical Work Permit** from L&I before starting, and the work must be inspected before it's covered or energized. Anyone you **hire** must be a licensed electrician.
- **Plumbing (your own residence)**: Under [RCW 18.106.150(1)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.106.150), a person is not required to hold a plumber's license to do plumbing on their **own residence**. There is no rent/sale/lease condition attached to it — the plumbing exemption is materially broader than the electrical one on that point. The one carve-out worth knowing: **medical gas piping** is excluded ([RCW 18.106.150(7)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.106.150)). A **plumbing permit and inspection are still required**, and anyone you hire to do plumbing for compensation must be licensed.
- **HVAC / gas piping**: Requirements vary by jurisdiction and scope — verify locally. Gas piping and mechanical work are often tied to plumbing or specialty rules.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Where the 24-month rule actually lives">

Nearly every owner-builder guide reports that Washington makes you promise to live in the house for 24 months before you can do your own wiring. That is a misreading of the statute, and it costs owner-builders work they're entitled to do.

Read [RCW 19.28.261(1)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.28.261) as two sentences. The **first** exempts an owner doing electrical work at their residence or "on other property owned by him or her" — flat, unconditional, no occupancy clause. The **second** is a separate rescue route for **"a new residential building with up to four units intended for rent, sale, or lease"** — the spec-house and rental case, which would otherwise fall outside the exemption entirely. *That* is where the 24-month owner-occupancy affidavit appears.

So: building a house on your own land to live in? You're covered by the first sentence and no affidavit is at issue. Building a duplex to rent out? You're in the second sentence, and the 24-month affidavit is the price of admission. Either way, confirm your specific situation with L&I before you pull the permit — they administer it.

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="Electrical permits come from L&I, not your county">

This trips up first-time owner-builders who assume one permit desk handles everything. Under [RCW 19.28.101(1)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.28.101), only **incorporated cities and towns** may run their own electrical inspection programs. **Counties cannot.** If you're building in unincorporated county land — which describes most rural owner-builder sites — your electrical permit and every electrical inspection come from **L&I**, on a separate application, separate fee schedule, and separate scheduling system from the county building permit.

Two hard rules follow ([RCW 19.28.101(4) and (5)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.28.101)): no electrical work may be **concealed** before it's approved, and **no utility may connect power** before approval. Cover your wiring early and you'll be opening walls back up.

</CalloutBox>

**Owner DIY allowed (non-trade work)**: Framing, roofing, concrete, drywall, painting, tile, flooring, and finish work — permitted and inspected per the building code.

**License/registration verification**: Washington Department of Labor & Industries — [lni.wa.gov](https://lni.wa.gov)

### Important Washington Requirement

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Worker protection: cover anyone you hire">

If you hire anyone (even day laborers), you must:

- Verify they have their own L&I coverage OR
- Add them to your coverage

Penalties for non-compliance are severe.

</CalloutBox>

### Sales Tax on Labor — the line item out-of-state guides miss

If you've built in a state where sales tax applies only to materials, budget for this one before it surprises you. Washington treats custom construction as a retail sale and charges retail sales tax on the **full contract price, labor included** — [WAC 458-20-170(4)(a)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=458-20-170). That's the 6.5% state rate plus your local rate, which in most of the Puget Sound region pushes the combined rate past 9%.

Practically: every contractor invoice you pay on a custom home carries sales tax on the whole invoice, not just the materials line. On $200,000 of subcontracted labor and materials in a 9.5% jurisdiction, that's roughly $19,000 you need in the budget from day one. The offsetting note for owner-builders is that work you perform yourself has no contract price to tax — you pay sales tax on the materials you buy at retail, and nothing on your own labor. It's one of the few places where the DIY savings are structural rather than just hourly.

Those are observed queue times. Washington also gives you **statutory** clocks and a vesting right, and knowing them changes how you file.

### Vesting: your application date locks the code

Under [RCW 19.27.095(1)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.27.095), a **complete** building permit application vests to the building and zoning ordinances in effect **on the date the application was filed** — not the date the permit issues. A code change or a new impact-fee ordinance that lands while your application sits in review does not reach back and apply to you. This is why filing a genuinely complete application ahead of a known code cycle is worth real money, and why "complete" is the word that carries all the weight.

### The 28-day completeness clock

[RCW 36.70B.070](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=36.70B.070) gives the department **28 days** to notify you in writing whether your application is complete and, if not, exactly what's missing. Miss that deadline and the application is **deemed complete on day 29**. Note what that does in combination with vesting: silence works in your favor.

### Decision deadlines and fee refunds

[RCW 36.70B.080](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=36.70B.080) sets final-decision deadlines running from the completeness determination — **65 days** for a project needing no public notice or hearing, **100 days** for one requiring notice but no hearing, and **170 days** for one requiring a public hearing. Excluded periods (your own resubmittal time, SEPA appeals) don't count against the clock. If the jurisdiction blows the deadline, it owes you a **10% permit-fee refund**, rising to **20%** for a further overrun. The refunds are small relative to a build budget, but the deadlines are the leverage — cite them when a file has gone quiet.

## Energy Code

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Among the strictest in the nation">

Washington has one of nation's strictest energy codes.

</CalloutBox>

### 2021 Washington State Energy Code

**More stringent than IECC**, Washington-specific requirements:

**Climate Zones**: Washington has exactly **two** climate zones — **4C** and **5B** ([WAC 51-11R-30100](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=51-11R-30100) Table R301.1). Every one of the 39 counties is assigned to one or the other. There is **no zone 6 anywhere in Washington**, a claim you'll see in guides that copy the national IECC map instead of reading the state table.

- **Zone 4C** (marine): western Washington — Seattle, Tacoma, Olympia, and the rest of the Puget Sound basin
- **Zone 5B**: eastern Washington — Spokane, Tri-Cities, Yakima — **plus Skamania County**, which is assigned 5B despite sitting on the west side of the Cascade crest

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="The prescriptive requirements are identical statewide">

Here's the part that saves you time: it does not matter which zone you're in. [WAC 51-11R-40213](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=51-11R-40213) Table R402.1.3 has a **single column** — "Climate Zone 5 and Marine 4" — covering the entire state. A wall assembly that works in Bellingham works in Walla Walla. If you've seen a Washington guide with separate requirement tables per zone, it's inventing a distinction the code doesn't make.

</CalloutBox>

If you've been planning around R-49 ceilings, re-check your truss heel height and your budget: **R-60 is the prescriptive number**, and hitting it over the exterior wall plate without compressing the batt usually means specifying raised-heel trusses at design time rather than discovering the problem at insulation inspection.

### The three mandatory tests

Washington does not let you talk your way past any of these, and all three are pass/fail with the certificate of occupancy behind them:

1. **Blower door — 4.0 ACH50** ([WAC 51-11R-40240](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=51-11R-40240)). This limit applies under **any** compliance path — prescriptive, UA-alternative, or performance. Unlike the IECC, Washington provides **no visual-inspection alternative**: there is no version of this where an inspector signs off on your air barrier by looking at it.
2. **Duct leakage** ([WAC 51-11R-40320](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=51-11R-40320)). Total leakage of **4.0 cfm per 100 sq ft** of conditioned floor area. The relaxed **8.0** figure applies only when the ducts and air handler are entirely **inside** the conditioned envelope — and note that a vented crawl space does **not** qualify as inside the envelope, which is where owner-builders most often assume they've earned the easier number and haven't.
3. **Ventilation airflow verification** ([WAC 51-11R-40350](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=51-11R-40350)). The whole-house ventilation system's airflow must be **measured** and verified, not merely installed to the manufacturer's rating.

Budget for a third-party testing contractor, and schedule the blower door with enough slack to fix a failure and retest. A failed test late in the build stops your CO.

### Washington-Specific Requirements

- **Duct insulation**: R-8 minimum (higher than most states)
- **Whole-house ventilation**: Required (moisture control), with airflow verified by measurement
- **High-efficiency heating**: Heat pumps encouraged, incentives
- **Triple-pane windows**: Common to meet code cost-effectively

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Energy code adds cost up front, pays back over time">

Energy code adds $15,000-$35,000 to construction costs vs. basic IRC, but long-term energy savings significant.

</CalloutBox>

## Seismic Requirements

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Most of western Washington is Seismic Design Category D">

Most of western Washington is Seismic Design Category D (some areas D2). Your exact category depends on site soils — check the Washington DNR Geologic Information Portal seismic design category map for your parcel.

</CalloutBox>

### Western Washington (Cascadia Subduction Zone)

**SDC D (commonly D2 on poorer soils)** throughout the Puget Sound region:
- Engineered foundation required
- Extensive shear wall requirements
- Hold-downs at all high-load points
- Continuous load path from roof to foundation
- Special inspection may be required

**Cost Impact**: $12,000-$30,000 for seismic engineering and construction

### Eastern Washington

Generally SDC C or D:
- Moderate seismic requirements
- Less stringent than west side

### Engineering Required

Nearly all Washington homes require structural engineering:
- Seismic design
- Foundation design
- Shear wall layout

**Cost**: $5,000-$15,000 for engineering

## Special Washington Considerations

### Wet Climate (Western WA)

**Rain management critical**:
- Gutters and downspouts essential
- Proper grading (6" drop in 10 feet)
- Drainage systems often required
- Waterproofing foundations (not just dampproofing)
- Roof overhangs important (24"+ recommended)

**Moisture Control**:
- Whole-house ventilation required
- Vapor barriers properly placed
- Dehumidification may be needed

### Landslide and Steep Slope Areas

Common in Puget Sound region:
- Geotechnical report required (steep slopes, known landslide areas)
- Cost: $3,000-$10,000
- Foundation design based on geo report
- May require deep foundations, retaining walls
- Some areas unbuildable

**Cost Impact**: $10,000-$50,000+ for challenging sites

Growing requirements:

## Water: the gate that stops rural builds before they start

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="No proof of water, no building permit">

This is the single most important thing on this page for anyone buying rural land in Washington, and it's the thing most out-of-state guides leave out entirely.

[RCW 19.27.097](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.27.097) requires you to submit **evidence of an adequate water supply** for the intended use of the building **before** the county will issue a building permit. And the statute closes the obvious loophole in one sentence: **"an application for a water right shall not be sufficient proof of an adequate water supply."** You cannot get a permit on the strength of a pending application. You need actual, demonstrated water.

Resolve water **before** you close on land, not after. A parcel where water can't be proven is a parcel you can't build on, and it will not look any different from a buildable one when you walk it.

</CalloutBox>

For most rural parcels, "evidence" means a permit-exempt domestic well under [RCW 90.44.050](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=90.44.050) — historically up to **5,000 gallons per day** without a water right. That baseline still applies in most of the state. But in 2018 the legislature responded to the *Hirst* decision by passing the streamflow restoration act, [Chapter 90.94 RCW](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=90.94), which sharply caps new permit-exempt withdrawals in **fifteen named watersheds** (WRIAs). If your parcel is in one of them, your well is metered against a much smaller number and you owe a fee at building permit:

The 950 gallon/day cap in the Puget Sound basins is the one to watch — it covers the Snohomish, Cedar-Sammamish, Duwamish-Green, Puyallup-White, Chambers-Clover, Deschutes, Kennedy-Goldsborough, and Kitsap watersheds, which is to say most of the rural land within commuting distance of Seattle and Tacoma. It's an **annual average**, so it's workable for an ordinary household, but it will not support irrigated acreage, livestock at scale, or a second dwelling. Read the caps as a limit on what you can *do* with the land, not just a form to file.

Statutory citations: [RCW 90.94.020](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=90.94.020), [RCW 90.94.030](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=90.94.030). Water rights outside the exemption are genuinely complex, especially in eastern Washington where senior rights are fully appropriated in many basins — get a water-rights attorney or a consultant involved before you buy, not after.

**Two rules that govern the electrical sequence** ([RCW 19.28.101(4) and (5)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.28.101)): nothing may be **concealed** until it has been approved, and **no utility connection** may be made until the work is approved. Insulators and drywall crews move fast — hold them until the rough electrical is signed off.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I save money as owner-builder in Washington?**
A: Yes, but savings are moderate (15-25%) due to high labor costs. You can do your own electrical and plumbing on property you own (with the proper permits and inspections — see the laws section), but most owner-builders still hire those trades. There's a second, quieter saving: because Washington taxes the full contract price of custom construction including labor, work you do yourself carries no sales tax on labor at all. Still worth it on expensive homes ($450K+).

**Q: Is financing available?**
A: Very difficult. Few WA lenders work with owner-builders. Local credit unions sometimes. Many owner-builders pay cash or use HELOC.

**Q: How much does energy code add?**
A: $15,000-$35,000 for enhanced insulation, better windows, duct sealing, and testing. But you save on utilities long-term.

**Q: Do I really need seismic engineering?**
A: Yes, for virtually all western WA homes. Cascadia earthquake is coming (geologists agree), and proper seismic design could save your life.

**Q: Should I build on a steep slope?**
A: Only if you budget $10,000-$50,000+ extra for geotechnical work, special foundations, and potentially retaining walls. Some sites not buildable at all.

## Final Thoughts

Washington owner-building requires commitment:
- **High costs** - Permits, fees, labor all expensive
- **Strict codes** - Energy and seismic add significant costs
- **Environmental regulations** - Can be complex and expensive
- **Long timelines** - Permitting takes months
- **Wet climate** - Western WA requires rain management

But rewards are significant:
- **Strong legal protections**
- **High home values** - Seattle area especially
- **Energy-efficient homes** - Low utility bills
- **Earthquake-resistant** - Peace of mind
- **Beautiful setting** - Mountains and water

Success factors:
1. **Budget generously** - Everything costs more in WA
2. **Hire engineers** - Required for seismic, often for site issues
3. **Trades** - Hired electrical and plumbing must be licensed; you may do your own on property you own, with the owner permits and inspections
4. **Prove water first** - RCW 19.27.097 blocks the building permit without evidence of adequate supply, and a pending water-right application does not count
5. **Environmental due diligence** - Research site thoroughly before buying
6. **Plan for rain** - Western WA, build during summer if possible

From the San Juan Islands to Spokane, Washington welcomes owner-builders who respect the codes and environment.
