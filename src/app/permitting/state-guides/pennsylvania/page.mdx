# Pennsylvania Owner-Builder Permit Guide

*By a retired general contractor with 15+ years building custom homes — [about the author](/about). Last updated: September 2026.*

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Quick Answer: Can You Build Your Own House in Pennsylvania?">

**Yes.** Pennsylvania has no statewide general contractor license, so you can act as your own general contractor on a home you own — the only state registration that touches residential work is [Home Improvement Contractor registration with the Attorney General](https://www.attorneygeneral.gov/resources/home-improvement-contractor-registration/), and that explicitly does **not** cover building a new home or owners working on their own property. Building codes come from the [Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC)](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dli/programs-services/labor-management-relations/bureau-of-occupational-and-industrial-safety/uniform-construction-code-home), which adopted the 2021 I-Codes effective January 1, 2026 and applies to new homes statewide — enforced either by your local building department or, in "opt-out" municipalities, by a certified third-party agency you hire. Pennsylvania does **not** license electricians or plumbers at the state level (a few cities like Philadelphia and Pittsburgh do), so whether you can do your own wiring or plumbing comes down to local rules — verify with your code official before you start.

</CalloutBox>

Pennsylvania has one of the most unusual regulatory landscapes in the country. The state's Uniform Construction Code (UCC) is adopted statewide — but a quirk in the law lets municipalities **opt out** of *running their own* building department. That does not switch the code off: in an opt-out municipality the UCC still applies to a new home, and the property owner simply hires a state-certified third-party agency to do the plan review and inspections instead of a municipal office. The result is a patchwork of *who* you deal with and how much it costs — but new residential construction is required to meet the UCC everywhere in the state.

For owner-builders, this matters enormously. Where you build still determines a lot — costs, timelines, which inspector you use, and zoning.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Opt-out does not mean no inspections">

Just don't assume "opt-out" means "no inspections" — it doesn't. The UCC and its inspections apply to a new home in every Pennsylvania municipality.

</CalloutBox>

## Pennsylvania Building Code Overview

<CalloutBox type="info" title="The Big Picture">

Pennsylvania's Uniform Construction Code is adopted **statewide** and applies to every new home — but the law lets a municipality opt out of *running its own* enforcement program, not out of the code itself.

</CalloutBox>

Pennsylvania adopted the Uniform Construction Code (UCC) through the [Pennsylvania Construction Code Act](https://www.legis.state.pa.us/WU01/LI/LI/US/HTM/1999/0/0045..HTM) (Act 45 of 1999, codified at 35 P.S. §§ 7210.101–7210.1103), with enforcement starting in 2004. The Pennsylvania Department of Labor & Industry (L&I) maintains the code, and the [UCC Review and Advisory Council (RAC)](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dli/programs-services/labor-management-relations/bureau-of-occupational-and-industrial-safety/uniform-construction-code-home/ucc-review-and-advisory-council) reviews each new ICC code edition on a triennial cycle.

### Current Code Adoption

Pennsylvania completed its triennial update to the 2021 I-Codes, effective **January 1, 2026**. The final-form regulation amending [34 Pa. Code § 403.21](https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pacode?file=%2Fsecure%2Fpacode%2Fdata%2F034%2Fchapter403%2Fs403.21.html) was adopted November 7, 2025 and published at **55 Pa.B. 7701**. (An earlier proposed rulemaking at 55 Pa.B. 1513 is still widely cited online; the final-form regulation is the one that governs.) As of 2026 the UCC adopts:

Pennsylvania historically lags the newest ICC cycle, so always confirm the locally adopted edition (and any local amendments) with the municipality or its third-party agency before you design. One warning on checking this yourself: as this guide was updated, L&I's own Uniform Construction Code home page still described the 2018 series with an effective date of February 14, 2022 — the previous cycle. The regulation governs, not the web page.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Pennsylvania does not adopt the NEC">

If you have been told to wire to the 2020 NEC, that is wrong — and it is a common error in published Pennsylvania advice. The phrases "NFPA 70" and "National Electrical Code" do not appear in 34 Pa. Code Chapter 403 at all. Residential wiring is governed by Chapters 34 through 43 of the 2021 IRC, which is why every Pennsylvania electrical amendment is written in E-section numbers. Quoting an NEC article number to a Pennsylvania inspector settles nothing.

</CalloutBox>

### The Pennsylvania Amendments That Override Your Code Book

This is the part that catches owner-builders working from a national IRC. Pennsylvania amends the model codes in **two** places, and the second one is the trap: some amendments live in the regulation at [34 Pa. Code § 403.21](https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pacode?file=%2Fsecure%2Fpacode%2Fdata%2F034%2Fchapter403%2Fs403.21.html), where you would expect them, and others live in the [Construction Code Act itself](https://www.legis.state.pa.us/WU01/LI/LI/US/HTM/1999/0/0045..HTM) at 35 P.S. § 7210.304, where almost nobody looks.

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="Wall bracing is enforced from the 2006 IRC">

This is the single most expensive line in the table, and it is the one nobody tells you. 35 P.S. § 7210.304(i) excludes the 2009 IRC wall bracing provisions **and any successor provisions**, and puts 2006 IRC R602.10 through R602.11.3 in force in their place. "Any successor provisions" is why the 2021 adoption did not change this — the exclusion follows the code forward.

Bracing changed substantially between 2006 and 2009. If your plans were drawn to the 2021 IRC bracing tables, or your framer is working from a current code book, you are building to a method Pennsylvania does not enforce and your reviewer is not checking against. Get the 2006 language in front of whoever draws your plans before the framing package is ordered.

</CalloutBox>

Everything else in IRC Chapters 34 through 43 — including all AFCI and GFCI requirements and service sizing — is the 2021 edition as published. The three receptacle carve-backs are narrow, but they cover three of the most routinely inspected items in a house, and a current code book will give the wrong answer on all three.

### The Recreational Cabin Exclusion

There is exactly one genuine no-UCC-permit path for a habitable building in Pennsylvania, and it is narrow. 35 P.S. § 7210.104(b)(7) excludes **recreational cabins** from the UCC — but "recreational cabin" is a defined term at § 7210.103, and the definition is a **seven-part test** where every part has to be true. Read it in full before you rely on it; it turns on how the building is used, owned and equipped, and a structure you intend to live in will not qualify.

It is also **not self-executing**. The owner has to file an affidavit or proof of insurance with the municipality to claim the exclusion — it is not a status you simply have. If you are building a genuine seasonal cabin, this is worth an hour with the statute. If you are hoping it covers a small house, it does not.

### Municipal Opt-Out — The Critical Distinction

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="Opting out is about who enforces, not whether the code applies">

Pennsylvania law lets a municipality opt out of *administering and enforcing* the UCC itself — it does **not** let the municipality switch the code off. L&I's own municipal elections table, read municipality by municipality in September 2026, holds **2,563 rows: 2,444 opted in (95.4%) and 119 opted out (4.6%)**. In an opt-out municipality the owner hires a state-certified third-party agency for plan review and inspections, and the UCC still applies to every new home.

</CalloutBox>

Pennsylvania law lets a municipality opt out of *administering and enforcing* the UCC itself — it does **not** let the municipality switch the code off. The Department of Labor & Industry publishes a table of every municipality and the election it made. Parsed row by row in **September 2026**, that [municipal elections table](https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/dli/documents/individuals/labor-management-relations/bois/documents/uccmun.htm) held **2,563 municipalities — 2,444 opted in (95.4%) and 119 opted out (4.6%)**, spread across 34 of the 67 counties, with no county entirely opted out. Check the table rather than a summary: a municipality may switch its election on **180 days' notice**, so the figure and your own township's status both move.

What opting out actually means:

- The UCC still applies in full to a new home — opting out does not exempt residential construction
- For **commercial** buildings, signs, and demolitions in an opt-out municipality, the **state L&I** is the code authority — under [34 Pa. Code § 403.103(g)](https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pacode?file=%2Fsecure%2Fpacode%2Fdata%2F034%2Fchapter403%2Fs403.103.html) the Department picks up only buildings *other than* one- and two-family dwellings
- For **residential** (one- and two-family) buildings in an opt-out municipality, the duty moves onto you. § 403.103(b) is explicit: "An applicant for a residential building permit shall obtain the services of a third-party agency certified in the appropriate categories to conduct the plan review and inspections." The agency does your **plan review** as well as your inspections, so it has to be engaged before you build, not when you are ready for a footing
- There is **no category of Pennsylvania municipality where a new home legally escapes UCC plan review and inspection**. L&I puts it plainly: "Certified third party agencies hired by property owners (or their contractors) enforce the residential requirements of the UCC in all opt-out municipalities"

What it means in practice:

- **Enforce-locally municipalities (most cities, boroughs, suburbs)**: The municipal building department (or a third-party agency the municipality retains) does your plan review and inspections
- **Opt-out municipalities (some rural townships)**: Same UCC, same inspections — but *you* select and pay a certified third-party agency (Code Inspections Inc., Commonwealth Code Inspection, Building Inspection Underwriters, etc.) directly, and the township still handles zoning, septic, and driveway permits

**Always verify** whether you deal with a municipal department or a third-party agency before you budget — but plan on full UCC inspections either way. If you are not sure which municipality a parcel is even in, the DCED [Munstats municipality finder](https://apps.dced.pa.gov/Munstats-Public/FindMunicipality.aspx) resolves an address to a municipality, which is the lookup the L&I table is keyed on.

### The Appeal You Give Up by Opting Out

This is the other half of the bargain, and it is not in the sales pitch for third-party agencies. **In an opt-out municipality there is no statutory forum to appeal a code decision at all.** Three provisions chain together to produce that result:

- A **board of appeals is required only of a municipality that has adopted a UCC ordinance** — 34 Pa. Code § 403.121(a). An opt-out municipality has adopted none, so no board exists
- The **right to appeal is expressly limited** to a municipality "which has adopted an ordinance" — 34 Pa. Code § 403.63(i)
- The **Industrial Board hears appeals of Department decisions**. In an opt-out municipality your decision-maker is not the Department, it is a private company you hired

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="You choose your inspector — and you give up the right to appeal the decision">

In an enforcing municipality, an adverse code decision goes to a board of appeals that must convene within 30 days and decide within five business days — and if it misses that deadline, "the appeal shall be deemed granted" ([35 P.S. § 7210.501(c)(5)](https://www.legis.state.pa.us/WU01/LI/LI/US/HTM/1999/0/0045..HTM)).

In an opt-out municipality none of that exists. Your only leverage is the contract you signed with the agency, so read it before you sign: what happens on a disputed call, whether a second reviewer can be assigned, and how you get out of the engagement.

</CalloutBox>

### Major City Codes

The state's largest cities operate independent enforcement and may have additional amendments:

- **Philadelphia**: Strict enforcement, additional Philadelphia-specific amendments, separate licensing for trades, mandatory architect for permits over a threshold
- **Pittsburgh**: Strict enforcement, additional zoning overlay districts (steep slope, hillside)
- **Allegheny County suburbs**: Generally enforce UCC strictly through municipal building departments

## Pennsylvania Owner-Builder Laws

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Where the freedom comes from">

Pennsylvania does not have a state general contractor license. Owner-builder rights are governed by local code interpretation and the UCC itself.

</CalloutBox>

### Legal Rights

You may act as your own general contractor on your own property because:

- Pennsylvania has no statewide GC license
- The UCC does not prohibit owner-builders — and you'll still pull a UCC permit and pass inspections
- Most municipalities allow homeowners to pull their own permits
- The [Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA), 73 P.S. § 517.1 et seq.](https://www.attorneygeneral.gov/resources/home-improvement-contractor-registration/) requires contractors performing $5,000+/year in home improvement work to register with the Attorney General — but it **does not apply** to:
  - Owners performing work on their own residences
  - New home construction (per the [Attorney General's HICPA FAQ](https://www.attorneygeneral.gov/resources/home-improvement-contractor-registration/contractor-frequently-asked-questions/), "the construction of a new home is not considered a home improvement under the law")

### Critical Restrictions and Requirements

**HICPA — What It Is and Isn't**:

- HICPA is a *registration* with the PA Office of Attorney General, **not a contractor license** — the AG notes registration "is not an endorsement, recommendation or approval... of the contractor's competency or skill"
- Applies to renovations, additions, and improvements to existing private residences (single-family homes and duplexes)
- Does NOT apply to new home construction (it's not a "home improvement")
- Does NOT apply to owners performing work on their own properties
- Provides consumer protections (mandatory contract terms, rescission rights, fund handling) — relevant when you *hire* a remodeler, not when you build new

**Trade Licensing in Pennsylvania**:

Per [PA L&I](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dli/programs-services/labor-management-relations/bureau-of-occupational-and-industrial-safety/uniform-construction-code-home/contractor-licensing), "the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania currently has no licensure or certification requirements for most construction contractors (or their employees)." That means no statewide license for:
- General contractors
- Electricians (though some cities like Philadelphia and Pittsburgh license locally)
- Plumbers (varies by city — Philadelphia and Pittsburgh license; many others don't)
- HVAC contractors

(The state *does* separately certify a few niche categories — asbestos/lead removal, manufactured-housing installers, crane operators — none of which apply to a typical owner-builder.) PA is one of the most license-light states in the country.

**Can you do your own electrical and plumbing?** There's no state trade license standing in your way, but two things still apply everywhere: (1) you generally need a UCC permit before you "erect, install, enlarge, alter, repair, remove, convert or replace" an electrical, gas, mechanical, or plumbing system ([34 Pa. Code § 403.62](https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pacode?file=%2Fsecure%2Fpacode%2Fdata%2F034%2Fchapter403%2Fs403.62.html); minor repairs like swapping a fixture or fixing a leak are exempt), and (2) some municipalities require licensed/registered trades regardless of who owns the home. Note the section number — §§ 403.61 through 403.66 are the **residential** permit provisions, and §§ 403.41 through 403.48 are the commercial ones. The permit language is nearly identical in both, which is why the commercial section gets cited at houses so often, including in published Pennsylvania guides. Whether a homeowner may pull the permit and do the work themselves is set locally — common in rural areas, restricted in Philadelphia and Pittsburgh.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Verify with your code official before wiring or plumbing">

No state trade license stands in your way, but you generally need a UCC permit first, and some municipalities require licensed or registered trades regardless of who owns the home. Verify with your code official before wiring or plumbing.

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Allegheny County: plumbing is not part of the UCC at all">

If you are building anywhere in Allegheny County, plumbing runs on a separate track. **35 P.S. § 7210.501(a.1)** bars a municipality in a county of the second class — which is Allegheny — from administering and enforcing the plumbing provisions of the UCC. The county enforces its own plumbing code instead, under the Local Health Administration Law.

That means a separate permit, a separate inspector and a separate rulebook from everything else on your job. Your building code official does not cover it and will not schedule it. Budget the extra permit and find out who your county plumbing inspector is before rough-in.

</CalloutBox>

**Insurance Considerations**:

- Workers' comp not required for owners working on their own property, but required if you hire any employees
- Most lenders require owner-builders to carry general liability and builder's risk insurance during construction
- Some specialty insurers offer owner-builder policies starting around $1,500–$3,000 for 12 months

### Seller Disclosure

The [PA Real Estate Seller Disclosure Law (68 Pa.C.S. Ch. 73, § 7301 et seq.)](https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/dos/department-and-offices/bpoa/real-estate/Seller%20Disclosure%20Law.pdf) requires sellers of one- to four-unit residential property to disclose known material defects, which in an owner-built home can include:

- Construction done without permits where permits were required
- Known code violations
- Material defects
- Septic and well issues

Knowingly failing to disclose a known material defect can create civil liability for misrepresentation — another reason to keep your UCC permits and inspection records.

Two notes on that Highway Occupancy Permit, because owner-builders routinely file the wrong form and then wait. The application you want for a house driveway is **M-950A**, the minimum-use driveway application, which covers a driveway expected to carry no more than 25 vehicles a day. And PennDOT has **60 days to act on it**: if the Department does not act within that window, the permit is deemed issued (35 P.S. § 7210.502(b)).

### The Statutory Permit Clock

Whatever your municipality's practice is, the review of your UCC building permit application has a deadline written into the Act. Under [35 P.S. § 7210.502(a)](https://www.legis.state.pa.us/WU01/LI/LI/US/HTM/1999/0/0045..HTM) and [34 Pa. - **15 business days** to grant or deny the application
- **5 business days** if a licensed design professional certifies that the plans meet the UCC — a real reason to have your architect or engineer sign the certification rather than just seal the drawings

One more thing the same section requires, and it is useful: the municipality must give you a **list of all other permits required** for your project. Read it as a checklist, not a guarantee — the statute says the municipality "will not be liable for the completeness of any list." Zoning, septic, driveway and stormwater are yours to chase regardless.

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="Your blower door target is 3.0 ACH50, not 5.0">

This is the most expensive error in circulation about Pennsylvania's energy code, and it is easy to make: the 2015 IECC that Pennsylvania used for years allowed 5.0 air changes per hour at 50 pascals, and a lot of published advice still prints that number.

Pennsylvania **did not amend IECC R402.4.1.2**, so the 2021 rule stands as written: **3 air changes per hour** in Climate Zones 3 through 8, which covers every square foot of the Commonwealth. There is no zone in Pennsylvania where 5.0 or 4.0 passes.

The reason it costs money is the sequencing. A house air-sealed to a 5.0 target and then tested at 3.0 fails at the end, after drywall, when the cheap fixes — sill sealing, sheathing seams, top-plate penetrations, the rim joist — are all buried. Air-seal to 3.0 from the first day of framing, and consider a mid-construction blower door test before insulation so a failure is still reachable.

</CalloutBox>

## Inspection Requirements

Pennsylvania is unusual in naming its required inspections in the statute rather than leaving them to local practice. **Five are required on every one- and two-family dwelling in the Commonwealth**, under [35 P.S. § 7210.501(e)(1)](https://www.legis.state.pa.us/WU01/LI/LI/US/HTM/1999/0/0045..HTM), repeated for enforcing municipalities at [34 Pa. Code § 403.64(d)](https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pacode?file=%2Fsecure%2Fpacode%2Fdata%2F034%2Fchapter403%2Fs403.64.html) and (f):

The statute adds the sequencing rule in the same sentence: "the final inspection shall not be deemed approved until all previous inspections have been successfully completed and passed." There is no path where a missed wallboard inspection gets waved through at the end.

**Your code official may run more than five.** 34 Pa. Code § 403.64(e) allows additional inspections at the official's discretion, and many municipalities publish a schedule of ten to twelve items — footing separated from foundation, each trade rough-in called separately, insulation, and separate finals per trade. That is normal and entirely lawful. Five is the statewide floor, not the expected total. Ask your municipality or agency for its own list in writing at permit issuance and build your schedule around that.

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="In an opt-out municipality, the length of that list is a price term">

If you are hiring a third-party agency, you are buying a list of inspections under a contract. A ten-item schedule and a five-item schedule are different amounts of work and different amounts of money — and different amounts of trip charges if the agency bills per visit.

Get the inspection list into the engagement letter before you sign, alongside scheduling turnaround and what a reinspection costs. This is the one term in the deal you can actually negotiate.

</CalloutBox>

Third-party agencies often offer faster scheduling than busy municipal departments — many will come out within a day or two of a call.

### Certificate of Occupancy

You cannot occupy the house without one. Under [34 Pa. Code § 403.65](https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pacode?file=%2Fsecure%2Fpacode%2Fdata%2F034%2Fchapter403%2Fs403.65.html), the certificate is due within **5 business days** of a compliant final inspection report — **10 business days in Philadelphia**.

One detail worth checking when it arrives: the certificate has to state **the edition of the construction code the building was judged against**. On a build that straddles a code cycle, that line is the record of which rules applied to your house, and it is the document a future buyer's inspector or your insurer will read. File it with your permits and inspection reports.

## Special Pennsylvania Considerations

### Mine Subsidence

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="Do not skip mine subsidence insurance in an undermined area">

Standard homeowner's policies exclude mine subsidence, which is why the Commonwealth runs its own program — the PA Mine Subsidence Insurance Program. Subsidence damage to a foundation is not a repair you want uninsured. Get a quote for your address before you close on the lot, not after the house is framed.

</CalloutBox>

Pennsylvania has a very large legacy of underground coal mining, with workings under significant portions of two regions:

- **Anthracite region** (Schuylkill, Carbon, Luzerne, Lackawanna, Northumberland)
- **Bituminous region** (Allegheny, Westmoreland, Washington, Fayette, Greene, Cambria counties)

You will see an acreage figure quoted for this — commonly "27 million acres," which cannot be right, since Pennsylvania's entire land area is roughly 28.6 million acres. Ignore the headline number and check your own parcel: DEP maintains mine maps and a subsidence risk lookup, and the answer that matters is whether there are workings under *your* lot, not how much of the Commonwealth is affected.

The premium figures that circulate online are unsourced too, so treat them the same way — call the program and get a quote for your address and coverage amount.

Site considerations:

- Pennsylvania DEP mine subsidence map review during plan review
- Foundation design that accommodates potential settlement (slab on grade preferred over basement in high-risk areas)
- Disclosure to lenders, insurers, and future buyers

### Radon

Pennsylvania has among the highest indoor radon levels in the country, and **the UCC does not require anything about it**. IRC Appendix F (radon-resistant construction) is an appendix, and 34 Pa. Code § 403.21(c) adopts no appendices — so there is no radon requirement anywhere in the Commonwealth, and no inspection will look for one. Do not assume Appendix F applies because your code book prints it.

That makes it entirely your call, and the economics are lopsided. A passive sub-slab depressurization rough-in — gas-permeable fill, a sealed membrane, a vent pipe from below the slab up through the roof — is a few hundred dollars while the slab is still open. Retrofitting an active mitigation system afterward runs several times that, and you cannot put the membrane back under a poured floor. DEP publishes radon guidance and county-level test data; check yours before the slab pour, not after.

### Fire Sprinklers

Also worth knowing affirmatively: **Pennsylvania does not require residential fire sprinklers.** 35 P.S. § 7210.304(g) excludes IRC R313.2 and its successor provisions, so a new one- or two-family dwelling needs no sprinkler system. The Act does keep a disclosure duty attached to it — a builder must **offer the sprinkler option in writing** at or before signing a purchase contract. Building for yourself, there is no one to make that offer to, but the requirement is why you will see sprinkler line items on Pennsylvania builder contracts.

### Termites

Pennsylvania falls in moderate-to-heavy termite probability zones. Treatment options include:

- Soil-applied termiticide (traditional, $400–$800)
- Bait systems
- Treated wood for framing in contact with concrete
- Pressure-treated bottom plates required at concrete contact

### Floodplains

PA has extensive floodplains along the Susquehanna, Delaware, Allegheny, Ohio, and Lehigh rivers. Floodplain construction triggers:

- Elevated lowest floor (typically BFE + 1 to 2 feet of freeboard)
- Special foundation (piers, columns, or wet-floodproofed walls below BFE)
- Flood insurance through NFIP or private alternatives ($1,500–$4,500/year)
- Higher review and inspection scrutiny

### Septic and Wells

Get the acronym right and you will find the right office: your permit comes from **your municipality's sewage enforcement officer**, not from a county and not from DEP. Under 25 Pa. Code §§ 72.21(a) and 72.24(a), the **local agency** — normally your township or borough, sometimes a joint agency serving several — employs or contracts a certified SEO, and **the permit application is made to that local agency**, on a DEP form. DEP's role is to write the standards (25 Pa. Code Chapter 73) and certify the officers; it does not normally issue your permit. A small municipality may share an SEO with its neighbors, so ask who covers your township rather than assuming there is an office in the building.

Sand mounds take real room, so check the layout on paper first: the free [Site Plan Studio](/site-plan-studio) draws the parcel to scale, dimensions well-to-system, and prints a letter-size sheet for the SEO. The isolation distances that usually decide whether a lot works are in 25 Pa. Code § 73.13 — from the **absorption area**, 100 ft to an individual water supply, 50 ft to surface water, 100 ft to mine subsidence areas, bore holes or sinkholes, and 10 ft to a property line, occupied building, pool or driveway. These are minimums, and § 73.13(a) lets the SEO require greater distances "if conditions warrant."

## Common Questions

**Do I need a license to build my own house in Pennsylvania?** No. Pennsylvania does not require a state general contractor license. Home Improvement Contractor (HICPA) registration doesn't apply to new construction or to owners working on their own property. You will still pull a UCC building permit and pass inspections.

**Can you build your own house without a permit in PA?** No — a new home needs a UCC permit and inspections everywhere in Pennsylvania. The only thing that changes is *who* issues it: your municipal building department, or (in an opt-out municipality) a state-certified third-party agency you hire. Separate zoning, septic, and driveway permits typically apply on top.

**What is the PA UCC opt-out?** It's a municipality choosing not to *run its own* code-enforcement program — not a way to skip the code. On L&I's own municipal elections table, read in September 2026, 2,444 of 2,563 municipalities (95.4%) enforce the UCC locally and 119 (4.6%) have opted out. In an opt-out municipality L&I handles commercial buildings and the owner hires a certified third-party agency for residential plan review and inspections. The UCC still applies to your new home either way — and a municipality can switch its election on 180 days' notice, so check the table rather than a summary.

**How many inspections does Pennsylvania require?** Five, named in the statute: foundation; plumbing, mechanical and electrical; frame and masonry; wallboard; and final (35 P.S. § 7210.501(e)(1)). The final cannot be approved until the other four have passed. Your code official may run additional inspections under 34 Pa. Code § 403.64(e), and many municipalities publish a list of ten or twelve, which is normal and lawful — but five is the statewide floor.

**How much does a PA owner-builder permit cost?** Variable. Roughly $1,500–$4,000 in low-cost rural townships (third-party UCC fees plus zoning/septic/driveway); $5,000–$10,000 in mid-size cities like Harrisburg and Allentown; $10,000–$22,000 in Philadelphia metro with impact fees. Note the prior "$500–$1,500 opt-out" figure assumed no UCC inspections, which isn't how PA works — budget for third-party plan review and inspection fees even in opt-out areas.

**Do I need licensed trades in Pennsylvania?** Not at the state level. Pennsylvania doesn't license electricians, plumbers, HVAC contractors, or general contractors statewide. However, Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, and some other cities require local trade certification.

**What is mine subsidence and do I need insurance?** Mine subsidence is ground settling caused by collapse of historical coal mining tunnels, concentrated in the anthracite region of northeast PA and the bituminous region of southwest PA. Standard homeowner's policies exclude it, which is why the Commonwealth runs its own program. Check DEP's mine maps for your specific parcel — the statewide acreage figures quoted online do not hold up — and get a premium quote for your address before you buy the lot.

## Final Thoughts for Pennsylvania Owner-Builders

Pennsylvania offers a wide range of regulatory environments — from Philadelphia's strict, heavily amended enforcement to rural opt-out townships where you hire your own inspection agency. Your single biggest decision is **where** you build.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="The UCC follows you everywhere">

Just remember the UCC and its inspections follow you to every municipality in the state.

</CalloutBox>

The big decisions:

1. **Municipal department vs. third-party agency**: In an opt-out municipality you choose and pay your own certified third-party agency for UCC plan review and inspections. That often means faster scheduling — but the same code, the same five statutory inspections, and **no board of appeals** if you disagree with a call. It is *not* a way to build without inspections.
2. **Get the Pennsylvania amendments in front of your designer**: wall bracing from the 2006 IRC, the gypsum membrane under most floor assemblies, and the three receptacle sections carried back to older editions. None of it is in a 2021 code book, and the bracing one is structural.
3. **Air-seal to 3.0 ACH50 from day one**: not the 5.0 that older Pennsylvania advice still prints. A blower door failure after drywall is the most expensive avoidable mistake on this list.
4. **Check the mine subsidence map**: especially in southwest and northeast PA. Standard policies exclude it; get a quote from the state program.
5. **Verify trade licensing locally**: PA doesn't license trades statewide, but Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, and some others do — and in Allegheny County, plumbing is a separate county permit entirely.
6. **Get the design snow load and frost depth in writing**: your building code official sets both, and roof framing requirements differ dramatically by elevation.
7. **Build a basement**: most PA markets expect them and they add value at low marginal cost.

Pennsylvania is exceptionally friendly to owner-builders who do their homework. Just don't assume the rules in one township apply to the next — they often don't.
