# Mississippi Owner-Builder Permit Guide

*By a retired general contractor with 15+ years building custom homes — [about the author](/about). Last updated: August 2026.*

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Quick Answer: Can You Build Your Own House in Mississippi?">

**Yes.** Mississippi licenses residential builders through the [Mississippi State Board of Contractors (MSBOC)](https://www.msboc.us/) once a job's cost exceeds $50,000 — but a homeowner building, or acting as their own general contractor on, their **own residence is specifically exempt** under Miss. Code Ann. § 73-59-15(1)(b). That exemption is something you actively use at the counter: § 73-59-17 requires the building official to refuse a permit unless the applicant furnishes evidence of being licensed **or exempt** — so proving your exemption is the recognized route to your permit, not a favor you ask for.

Three things catch owner-builders here. **First, the exemption is yours and nobody else's**: since HB 1163 (2022), every electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and HVAC subcontractor on a residential project needs an MSBOC license *no matter the dollar amount* — there is no small-job threshold, and the sub who sets your temporary power pole for $700 is covered. **Second, permits are now mandatory statewide**: since July 1, 2022 every county and municipality must require a building permit as a condition of construction, whether or not it enforces a building code. **Third, whether a *code* applies to your parcel was settled by a local vote years ago** — a one-time 120-day opt-out that closed November 30, 2014 for most of the state, and a 60-day opt-out in 2006 for the five coastal counties and the municipalities inside them. That vote is in your board's minute book. No statewide roster of opt-outs was ever published, so reading the minutes is the only way to know.

</CalloutBox>

Mississippi is one of the most decentralized owner-builder states in the country, but not in the way it is usually described. For decades it had no statewide residential building code at all. After Hurricane Katrina (2005) the Legislature pushed codes onto the Gulf coast, and in 2014 it directed the rest of the state to adopt one. Both times it attached the same escape hatch: a single, short window in which a local government could pass a resolution voting itself out — 60 days on the coast in 2006, 120 days statewide in 2014. Both windows closed and neither has reopened. Nothing about your parcel's code status is being decided today; it was decided by a board of supervisors or a board of aldermen more than a decade ago, and the record of that vote sits in a minute book at the courthouse.

The result is a genuine patchwork: a tightly inspected build in suburban DeSoto or coastal Harrison County, and a build with almost no code oversight two counties over. What is *not* patchwork anymore is permitting. Since July 1, 2022 every county and municipality in Mississippi has been required to demand a building permit before construction, code or no code. This guide separates the legal framework (statewide, and more uniform than the folklore admits) from the enforcement reality (intensely local).

## Mississippi Building Code Overview

<CalloutBox type="info" title="The Big Picture">

**There is no statewide residential code edition, and there never was one.** What state law does is narrower and more useful to know: it tells a jurisdiction *which* editions it may choose from if it enforces a code at all, it excluded residential fire sprinklers from that choice, and — twice, in 2006 and 2014 — it gave local governments a brief window to vote themselves out of the requirement entirely. Both windows are closed. Permitting, separately, is now mandatory everywhere.

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Your parcel's code status was decided by a vote, not by a policy that's still evolving">

This is the single most misunderstood point about Mississippi, and almost every online guide gets it backwards. State law does not say counties "may decide whether to enforce a code." **SB 2378 (2014) § 1(1) says jurisdictions *shall* adopt one of the last three editions** — and then § 1(3) gave each of them exactly 120 days to pass a resolution opting out. That window closed on November 29-30, 2014. The State Fire Marshal's own page put it plainly: local governments "must enact uniform building codes unless they opt out prior to Nov. 30, 2014."

The coast works the same way one cycle earlier: **HB 1406 (2006) § 1(4) gave each of the five coastal counties, and each municipality within them, 60 days to opt out by resolution** — identical language, identical mechanism.

So the question is never "has my county gotten around to adopting a code?" It is **"how did my county vote in 2014 — or in 2006, on the coast?"** That vote is recorded in the board of supervisors' or board of aldermen's minute book. **No official roster of opt-outs was ever published by the state**, so the minute book is the primary source. Ask the chancery clerk or the city clerk for the minutes covering the relevant window.

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="Permits are mandatory statewide, code or no code — since July 1, 2022">

The most consequential change in Mississippi owner-builder law in twenty years got almost no attention. **HB 1163 (2022) amended § 19-5-9 (counties, subsection (11)) and § 21-19-25 (municipalities, subsection (6)) to require that "each and every" county and municipality require permitting as a condition of construction — regardless of whether it has adopted a building code.**

This kills the "rural Mississippi doesn't need permits" folklore. A jurisdiction may genuinely have no code, no plan review, and no inspectors, and *still* be under a statutory duty to require a permit before you build. The Legislature tried to walk it back in 2023 — SB 2612 would have let jurisdictions opt out of mandatory permitting — and **the bill was vetoed**.

In practice, enforcement varies and some rural offices are still catching up. Do not plan around that gap. Call the county, ask what their permit process is, and get the answer in writing.

</CalloutBox>

### The Coastal Mandate — and Its Opt-Out

HB 1406 (2006), passed in the wake of Katrina, named **five** counties: **Jackson, Harrison, Hancock, Stone, and Pearl River** — them and every municipality within them — and required adoption and enforcement of the wind and flood mitigation provisions of the international codes.

But the coastal mandate is not, and never was, absolute. **§ 1(4) of the same bill gave each of those counties and each of those municipalities 60 days to opt out by resolution**, in language essentially identical to the statewide opt-out the Legislature would write eight years later. So "the coastal counties are required to enforce" is as wrong as "the inland counties get to decide." Both are frozen votes from a window that closed. Pull the minutes.

<CalloutBox type="info" title="George County: settled, not ambiguous">

George County is **not** a coastal-mandate county. It gets grouped with the coast in insurance discussions and in a lot of secondary writing, and in 2007 the Legislature actually took up the question — **SB 2853 would have added George County to the statute, and it died in committee.** The coastal statute names five counties and George is not one of them. (Like every other non-coastal jurisdiction, George County was covered by the 2014 statewide requirement and its 120-day opt-out window.)

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Confirm status in writing before assuming you're unregulated">

In unincorporated rural Mississippi it is entirely possible that nobody will review your plans or inspect your framing. What is *not* safe to assume is that no permit is required — since 2022 the county is under a statutory duty to require one. And an absence of inspection does not relieve you of liability, does not satisfy a lender, and does not help you at resale.

Two calls settle it: ask the chancery clerk (county) or city clerk for the minute-book entry covering the 2014 opt-out window, and ask the building department what their permit process is for a one- and two-family dwelling. Build to a current IRC edition either way.

</CalloutBox>

## Mississippi Owner-Builder Laws

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Where the freedom comes from">

Mississippi licenses residential **builders**, but the homeowner building their own home is carved out of that licensing requirement by statute. That exemption — not the absence of a license law — is the legal basis for building your own house here.

</CalloutBox>

The [Mississippi State Board of Contractors (MSBOC)](https://www.msboc.us/) licenses residential work under Miss. Code Ann. Title 73, Chapter 59 (the Residential Builders and Remodelers law). Two definitions in § 73-59-1 set the dollar thresholds people quote:

- A **residential builder** is someone who builds a one- to three-story dwelling for another person when the total cost of the work exceeds **$50,000** (§ 73-59-1(b)).
- A **residential remodeler** is someone who improves an existing residence when the cost exceeds **$10,000** (§ 73-59-1(c)).

Both of those are *builder* thresholds — they define who counts as a builder or remodeler needing that license. **Neither one is a trade threshold.** Confusing the $10,000 remodeler figure with a minimum for electrical, plumbing, or HVAC licensing is the most common and most dangerous error written about Mississippi law, and the section on trade contractors below explains why.

### The Owner-Builder Exemption

Miss. Code Ann. § 73-59-15(1) lists who the chapter does not apply to. Paragraph (b) is yours, and here it is in full:

> Any person who undertakes construction or improvement on his own residence, or who acts as his own general contractor in the performance of construction or improvement on his own residence

That is the entire paragraph. Note what is **not** in it: there is no "provided the property is not being built for the purpose of sale, lease, or rent" proviso attached to (1)(b). Widely copied versions of this exemption append that condition inside the quotation marks. It isn't in the statute, and the distinction matters — a condition would disqualify you outright, whereas what Mississippi actually wrote is a presumption you can rebut.

The structure is worth understanding, because it is what actually governs you:

- **§ 73-59-15(1)(b)** — the owner-builder exemption. Unconditional on its face: your own residence, whether you swing the hammer or act as your own GC.
- **§ 73-59-15(1)(c) and (1)(d)** — separate exemptions (including building for a relative) that *do* carry not-for-sale, not-for-rent conditions in their own text.
- **§ 73-59-15(2)** — the backstop. Rather than conditioning (1)(b) directly, the Legislature created a **rebuttable presumption** that you were building for the purpose of sale if you exceed the volume limit described below.

In plain English: **you do not need an MSBOC residential builder license to build your own home, no matter what it costs**, and you may act as your own general contractor and hire your own subcontractors. Your risk is not a hidden condition in paragraph (b); it is the presumption in subsection (2).

### Your Right at the Permit Counter — § 73-59-17

This is the most practically useful provision in the whole chapter and almost nobody quotes it. **Miss. Code Ann. § 73-59-17 directs the building official to *refuse* to issue a permit unless the applicant furnishes evidence that they are either licensed under Chapter 59 or exempt from it.** The same section directs officials to report suspected violators to the Board.

Read it from your side of the counter: your exemption is not something you plead for. It is the alternative the statute puts on equal footing with a license, and furnishing evidence of it is what entitles you to the permit. If a clerk tells you a homeowner cannot pull a permit for a new house, § 73-59-17 is the citation — it presumes exactly that transaction and tells the official how to handle it.

Bring proof the residence is yours: the recorded deed, the tax parcel record, and a plain statement that you are claiming the § 73-59-15(1)(b) exemption as the owner of the property.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="The one-home limit runs on a rolling year — not a calendar year">

Mississippi's owner-builder exemption is not unlimited, and the way the clock runs trips people up.

**§ 73-59-15(2) says "within a period of one (1) year."** That is a **rolling twelve months** measured from each permit or each residence — not a calendar year that resets on January 1. HB 1163 (2022) cut the allowance from two dwellings to one.

The trap is obvious once you see it: pull a permit in **December** and another the following **January** and you have used two in about six weeks. Those are different calendar years and it does not help you — the two permits are inside the same rolling twelve months, and that is what the statute measures.

Two things this rule is *not*. It is not a cap on how many houses you may ever build for yourself — wait out the twelve months and you are clear. And exceeding it is **not an automatic violation**: it creates a **rebuttable presumption** that you were building for the purpose of sale. You can rebut it with evidence you actually built to live there — occupancy, homestead exemption, a homeowner's rather than builder's-risk policy, a mortgage on the property as a residence. Build one, live in it, and you never reach the argument.

</CalloutBox>

### The § 73-59-15(1)(g) Two-Residence Provision — and Why You Shouldn't Plan Around It

**§ 73-59-15(1)(g)** allows **two** residences per year where **no building permit is required** for the construction. On its face it is a rural-Mississippi accommodation.

Be careful with it. Since HB 1163 (2022) made permitting mandatory in every county and municipality via §§ 19-5-9(11) and 21-19-25(6), it is genuinely unclear whether any jurisdiction in Mississippi still satisfies (1)(g)'s predicate — the provision may be describing a category the Legislature subsequently emptied. Nobody should build a second house in a twelve-month period on the strength of it. Mentioned here for completeness; treat the one-per-rolling-year rule as your operating limit.

### A Related Exemption: Building for Family

Mississippi also exempts a person who builds, or acts as general contractor, for an owner-occupant **related by blood or direct marriage (consanguinity or affinity)**, where the property won't be for sale, rent, or public use. Unlike (1)(b), this exemption carries those not-for-sale conditions in its own text. If you're building a home for a parent or child who will live in it, this exemption may cover you — confirm the details with the MSBOC.

### Penalties for Building Unlicensed (When the Exemption Doesn't Apply)

If you fall outside the exemption — e.g., you build a $250,000 spec house for resale without a license — the consequences come in two flavors, and the civil one usually hurts more.

**Criminal.** Miss. Code Ann. § 73-59-9 makes unlicensed residential contracting a **misdemeanor** punishable by a fine of **not less than $100 and not more than $5,000**, or imprisonment of **30 to 60 days**, or both.

**Civil — you cannot enforce your own contract.** § 73-59-9(2) bars an unlicensed person from bringing an action to enforce the contract. The most they can recover is **"actual documented expenses,"** and those must be proven **"by clear and convincing evidence"** — the demanding standard, not the ordinary preponderance one. Profit, overhead, and the agreed price are simply gone.

**No lien, either.** Under § 85-7-403(5), an unlicensed contractor gets no construction lien — and neither does anyone who contracted with an unlicensed contractor. That second clause is the one to notice: it can reach *you*, through the sub you hired.

### Licensed Trade Contractors (If You Hire Out)

This is where Mississippi differs sharply from a true no-license state, and where the guidance you'll find elsewhere is most often wrong in the permissive direction.

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="There is no dollar threshold for residential trade licensing. It is zero.">

**Miss. Code Ann. § 73-59-3(1)(d)**, added by **HB 1163 (2022)** effective July 1, 2022, requires an MSBOC license for subcontractors **"of any tier"** performing electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and/or HVAC work on any residential project — **"no matter the dollar amount of the construction or improvements."**

Not $10,000. Not any number. **Zero.** The sub who sets a $700 temporary power pole needs a license. The plumber who does a half-day of rough-in needs a license. The handyman who moves one circuit for you needs a license.

If you have read that residential trade work requires a license "of $10,000 or more," you have encountered the **$10,000 remodeler threshold from § 73-59-1(c)** — a definition of who counts as a *remodeler* — misapplied to the trades. Building around that number means hiring unlicensed subs on the belief you're under a limit that does not exist.

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Your exemption covers you. It does not travel to anyone you hire.">

§ 73-59-15(1)(b) exempts **you**, personally, working on **your own** residence. It confers nothing on your subs. Every electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and HVAC contractor on your job needs their own MSBOC license, at any contract price, and the fact that the homeowner is exempt is not a defense for them.

This matters to you and not only to them: the lien bar in § 85-7-403(5) reaches parties who contracted with an unlicensed contractor, unlicensed work is a disclosure problem at resale, and an insurer or lender that finds unlicensed trade work on a new build will have opinions. Verify every sub at the [MSBOC license search](https://www.msboc.us/) before you sign, and keep a copy of the license record in your job file.

</CalloutBox>

### If You Hire a Construction Manager or Owner's Rep

Owner-builders frequently want a professional to run the schedule and the subs while the owner holds the permit. Mississippi drafted directly at that arrangement.

**§ 73-59-3(1)(c)** requires a license for a person providing construction management services for compensation **"no matter if that owner himself is the general contractor or a holder of a building permit."** The clause is written to reach exactly the consultant an owner-builder hires — being your own permit holder does not make your paid manager exempt.

And you are not accidentally caught by it yourself: **§ 73-59-1(h)** defines construction management in a way that **excludes the owner by name**. So you may manage your own build; the person you pay to help you manage it needs a license.

### Homeowner Doing Their Own Trade Work

Here the answer depends entirely on local ordinance, and the state licensing chapter has nothing to say about it.

**Chapter 59 does not restrict you.** § 73-59-15(1)(b) disapplies the entire chapter — every licensing requirement in it — to a person working on their own residence. There is no provision in the MSBOC residential law designating electrical, HVAC, or fuel gas as trades a homeowner may not self-perform. Any claim that "state law treats these as life-safety trades" is not traceable to the statute.

**Local ordinances are where real restrictions live.** Counties act under **Miss. Code Ann. § 19-5-9** — note that county building-code authority reaches **unincorporated areas only** — and municipalities under **§ 21-19-25**. A city or county may, by ordinance, require that electrical or mechanical work be performed by a licensed contractor even on an owner-occupied home. Some do; some don't; the ordinance text is the only reliable source.

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Ask for the ordinance, not the opinion">

When you call the building department, don't ask "can I do my own wiring?" — you'll get one clerk's understanding. Ask: **"Which ordinance section covers homeowner self-performed work, and can I get a copy?"** If they cite a state law, ask which one; there is no state statute restricting an owner working on their own residence, and the question usually surfaces a local ordinance or an internal policy that isn't an ordinance at all.

Two more practical notes. If your parcel is inside city limits, the city ordinance governs, not the county's. And in a jurisdiction with an ordinance requiring licensed trades, the ordinance controls regardless of your Chapter 59 exemption — the exemption answers a state licensing question, not a local one.

</CalloutBox>

### Liability and Insurance

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="As owner-builder, the liability is yours">

As an owner-builder in Mississippi:

- You're personally liable for any injuries on-site (workers' comp is recommended any time you pay labor)
- You can usually obtain builder's risk insurance, but rates run higher than for a licensed builder
- Most construction lenders require owner-builders to carry liability and builder's risk coverage during the build
- On the coast, **windstorm and flood insurance** are major cost factors — and building to code is what makes them obtainable and affordable

</CalloutBox>

### Seller Disclosure

Mississippi requires a **Property Condition Disclosure Statement (PCDS)** under Miss. Code Ann. §§ 89-1-501 to 89-1-527 whenever residential property of one to four dwelling units is sold with the help of a licensed real estate broker or salesperson. The form is promulgated by the [Mississippi Real Estate Commission (MREC)](https://www.mrec.ms.gov/) and must be delivered to the buyer before they sign. An owner-built home doesn't have to be labeled as such, but any known defects, unpermitted work, or code issues must be disclosed.

## Energy Code Requirements

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Light energy code, hot-humid climate">

Mississippi has **no separate statewide energy-code adoption.** Whatever energy provisions apply to you arrive through the IRC edition your jurisdiction adopted — so a county on the 2018 IRC, one on the 2021, and one on the 2024 are working from different IECC vintages, and a jurisdiction that enforces no code has no energy requirement at all. Ask which IRC edition yours adopted; that answers the energy question too.

Either way the state sits entirely in hot-humid climate zones, and the real challenge is **cooling and humidity control**, not heating.

</CalloutBox>

### Foundation and Frost Depth

Mississippi's frost depth is shallow — the state's design frost penetration is roughly **6 inches or less**, so deep frost footings are not the driver here that they are up north. The IRC minimum footing depth (12 inches below undisturbed grade) governs in practice. The bigger foundation concerns in Mississippi are **expansive clay (Yazoo clay)** and **coastal flood elevation** — covered below.

## Special Mississippi Considerations

### Hurricanes and High Wind (Gulf Coast)

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="The coast is a different building world">

Hancock, Harrison, and Jackson counties face direct Gulf hurricane exposure. Building there means engineered wind resistance, a continuous load path, wind-borne-debris protection, and elevated floors in flood zones.

Whether these are legally required on your parcel depends on whether your county or municipality took the HB 1406 (2006) opt-out — check the minutes. Two things don't depend on that vote: floodplain requirements flow from your jurisdiction's NFIP participation rather than the building code, and the storm doesn't read resolutions. Build to the coastal provisions on the coast regardless of what the minute book says.

</CalloutBox>

On the Mississippi coast, design wind speeds run high and the wind-borne-debris region requires protected openings. Plan for:

- **Continuous load path**: hurricane straps/clips tying roof-to-wall-to-foundation, engineered to the IRC/IBC uplift requirements
- **Wind-borne-debris protection**: impact-rated windows/doors or code-approved shutters/panels over all glazed openings
- **Roof**: enhanced sheathing nailing schedules, sealed roof deck, and rated shingles/metal
- **Flood elevation**: in a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area, the lowest floor must be elevated to or above the Base Flood Elevation (often on piers/pilings near the water), with a flood elevation certificate

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Wind mitigation can earn an insurance discount">

Mississippi law (Miss. Code Ann. § 83-75-1, administered through the Mississippi Insurance Department) provides **windstorm-mitigation insurance discounts** for new coastal homes built to the higher wind-resistance standards. The same connectors and shutters that the code requires can lower your premium — keep your inspection documentation and ask your insurer about the credit. The Mississippi Insurance Department maintains a uniform building-code/wind-mitigation resource at the [MID website](https://www.mid.ms.gov/).

</CalloutBox>

### Tornadoes (Statewide)

Mississippi is deep in Dixie Alley and gets violent, often nighttime tornadoes — frequently more deadly than Plains tornadoes because they hit at night and in forested terrain. The IRC doesn't require storm shelters, but strongly consider:

- A reinforced safe room or above-ground shelter built to **FEMA P-361 / ICC 500** standards
- A 5x7 interior reinforced room or a below-grade shelter
- Cost: roughly $4,000-$10,000 for a basic in-home shelter — cheap insurance in this state

### Termites — Including Formosan on the Coast

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Mississippi is a 'very heavy' termite zone — treatment is code-required">

The entire state falls in the IRC's "very heavy" termite-infestation region, so the code requires protection against subterranean termites in new construction (soil treatment, bait systems, or approved physical barriers). On the coast, the aggressive **Formosan subterranean termite** raises the stakes further.

</CalloutBox>

Mississippi has both native subterranean termites statewide and the invasive Formosan subterranean termite, which per Mississippi State University Extension is now established in at least part of **26 Mississippi counties**, concentrated on the Gulf coast and spreading north. Formosan colonies are enormous and fast — they can do serious structural damage quickly. Budget for:

- A pre-slab/pre-construction **soil termiticide treatment** ($300-$800), required by code in enforced jurisdictions and strongly recommended everywhere
- Or an approved baiting system / physical barrier as an alternative
- Borate-treated framing lumber on the coast for added protection
- A termite-protection certificate, which buyers and lenders will want

### Expansive Clay (Yazoo Clay)

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Yazoo clay can destroy a slab — get a soils report in the Jackson area">

Central Mississippi — especially the Jackson metro (Hinds, Madison, Rankin) — sits over **Yazoo clay**, a notoriously expansive soil that swells and shrinks with moisture and cracks foundations. A geotechnical evaluation is strongly recommended for any slab-on-grade in this region.

</CalloutBox>

Where expansive clay is present, plan for:

- A geotechnical soils report before foundation design
- An engineered foundation (stiffened/post-tensioned slab, or deep footings/piers to stable strata)
- Careful site drainage and consistent moisture management around the perimeter
- Avoiding large grade changes and big trees too close to the foundation

### Wastewater and Septic — Run This First

Most guides treat septic as a line item. In Mississippi it is a **sequencing** problem, and getting the order wrong is one of the few mistakes here that can strand a finished house. Title 41, Chapter 67 of the Mississippi Code gives the Mississippi State Department of Health (MSDH) two levers, and the second one is the sharp one.

<CalloutBox type="critical" title="The Notice of Intent comes before the house — and the water meter is the enforcement">

**Lever one — § 41-67-5(1): file a Notice of Intent *before* you construct the residence.** Read the trigger carefully. It is not "before you install a septic system." It is **before constructing the residence**. Your wastewater filing belongs at the front of your schedule, alongside the soil evaluation and before you turn dirt — not somewhere in the middle after framing.

**Lever two — § 41-67-5(2): no public water utility may connect you without prior written health-department approval.** This is the mechanism that gives the first one teeth. The health department does not have to stop your build; it only has to not sign, and then no water association, district, or municipal utility may lawfully set your meter. A finished house with no water is not a house you can occupy, close on, or insure.

Violations carry a **$500 fine and shutoff of the water supply** under § 41-67-28(5)(a).

The practical rule: **the wastewater approval gates the water meter, and the water meter gates occupancy.** Sequence your build around that, not around the building permit.

</CalloutBox>

Since the filing comes before the house, have the layout drawn when you file — the free [Site Plan Studio](/site-plan-studio) puts the lot, the well and the wastewater system on a to-scale sheet, measures the separations, and prints who sets those distances rather than inventing a number.

#### The Two-Acre Affidavit Exemption — and the 22 Counties That Override It

**§ 41-67-6(7)** lets a lot of **two acres or more** be exempt from department approval by affidavit. Three conditions must all hold:

1. The wastewater is **contained entirely on the lot**
2. **No watercourse** is impacted
3. The **installer files an affidavit** attesting to the installation

It is a real exemption and a genuinely useful one on acreage. But do not treat it as a statewide free pass:

- **22 counties override it by local ordinance**, requiring MSDH Final Approval anyway. Whether yours is among them is a phone call to the county health department, and it is a call worth making before you buy the lot.
- **Water associations and lenders impose their own requirements.** An association that wants Final Approval before setting a meter can insist on it whether or not the statute exempts you, and a construction lender can condition a draw on it. The affidavit route exempts you from the *department*; it does not exempt you from the counterparty holding the thing you need.

If you are on acreage and planning to use the affidavit route, confirm all three parties — county, water association, lender — before you rely on it.

## Common Questions

**Do I need a license to build my own house in Mississippi?** No. Miss. Code Ann. § 73-59-15(1)(b) exempts a homeowner who builds, or acts as general contractor on, their own residence from the residential builder license — even though that license is otherwise required for jobs over $50,000. And § 73-59-17 puts that exemption to work at the counter: the building official must refuse a permit unless the applicant shows they are licensed **or exempt**. You can act as your own GC and hire your own subs.

**Can you build your own house without a permit in Mississippi?** No — not lawfully, anywhere in the state. Since July 1, 2022, HB 1163 has required every county and municipality to demand a permit as a condition of construction regardless of code adoption (§§ 19-5-9(11), 21-19-25(6)); a 2023 bill to allow opting out of that was vetoed. What varies is whether there is a *code* to review your plans against and inspectors to check the work. Some rural offices are still catching up in practice, but "no permit needed out here" is folklore, not law.

**What is the Mississippi owner-builder exemption?** It's the carve-out in § 73-59-15(1)(b) that lets you build or GC your own residence without an MSBOC license, at any cost. The limit: build more than one residence, or pull more than one residential permit, **within any rolling twelve months**, and § 73-59-15(2) creates a rebuttable presumption that you were building for sale.

**Can a homeowner do their own electrical and plumbing in Mississippi?** State licensing law doesn't restrict you at all — § 73-59-15(1)(b) disapplies the entire MSBOC chapter to an owner working on their own residence. Restrictions, where they exist, are **local ordinances** adopted under § 19-5-9 (counties, unincorporated areas) or § 21-19-25 (municipalities). Ask your building department for the ordinance section covering homeowner self-performed work, not for an opinion.

**How much does a Mississippi owner-builder permit cost?** In jurisdictions with valuation-based fees, building plus trade permits commonly land in the low four figures for a 2,000 sq ft home, with water/sewer taps often exceeding the permit fees themselves. These are illustrative ranges — Mississippi fee schedules are local and change without notice, so get the current number from your building department.

**Do the subs I hire need a license even on small jobs?** Yes, at any dollar amount. § 73-59-3(1)(d) requires MSBOC licensing for electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and HVAC subcontractors "of any tier" on residential projects "no matter the dollar amount." There is no threshold — not $10,000, not any figure.

**Which Mississippi counties are best for owner-builders?** DeSoto for resale and speed, Madison and Rankin for the Jackson metro, Lamar for affordability with amenities. The coast (Harrison/Hancock) only if you'll budget for wind, flood, and insurance.

## Final Thoughts for Mississippi Owner-Builders

Mississippi is a state of two extremes for owner-builders. In suburban DeSoto, Madison, or Rankin — or anywhere on the coast that didn't opt out in 2006 — you get a clear IRC edition, fast permitting, and a by-the-book process at low fees. In much of rural Mississippi you may face no plan review and no inspection at all, which is freedom and rope in equal measure. What you never face, anywhere, is a lawful path to building with no permit.

The big decisions:

1. **Establish your parcel's code status from the public record before you buy the lot**: Not "does the county enforce a code?" but "how did this county vote in its 2014 opt-out window — or its 2006 window, on the coast?" Ask the chancery clerk or city clerk for the minute-book entry. The state never published a roster, so this is the primary source, and the answer shapes your entire budget.
2. **Stay inside the one-per-rolling-year limit**: Build one home, occupy it. The clock is a rolling twelve months, not a calendar year — a December permit and a January permit are two permits in the same year for these purposes. Exceeding it creates a rebuttable presumption you were building for sale, not an automatic violation, but don't put yourself in the position of rebutting it.
3. **Every trade sub needs a license, at any price**: § 73-59-3(1)(d) requires MSBOC licensing for electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and HVAC subcontractors of any tier "no matter the dollar amount." There is no $10,000 floor and no small-job exception — the sub setting your temporary power pole is covered. Your exemption is personal to you and does not travel to anyone you hire; verify every license before you sign.
4. **Sequence the wastewater approval first**: The Notice of Intent is due before you construct the residence, and the health department's approval is what lets a public utility set your water meter. Get that order wrong and you can finish a house you cannot occupy.
5. **Build to code even where no one's checking**: In a county with no adopted edition, a current IRC is still the standard your insurer and future buyer expect. Hire your own inspections.
6. **Respect the hazards**: termites (treat the soil), Yazoo clay (engineer the foundation), and on the coast, wind and flood (which also drive your insurance).

Mississippi rewards the owner-builder who does their homework on **location** — and here that homework is unusually concrete. Two calls to a clerk and a building department will tell you more about your build than any guide can, because the answer was written into a minute book years ago and never republished.

*Licensing. Miss. Code Ann. § 73-59-15(1)(b) exempts a person who undertakes construction or improvement on his own residence, or who acts as his own general contractor in the performance of construction or improvement on his own residence. That paragraph is reproduced verbatim in the body of this guide, and it does not contain the not-for-sale proviso that is commonly and incorrectly appended to it inside quotation marks; those conditions belong to (1)(c) and (1)(d), and the sale purpose appears in the subsection (2) presumption. § 73-59-15(2) creates a rebuttable presumption of building for sale on exceeding one residence or one residential permit within a period of one year, a rolling twelve months, cut from two dwellings to one by HB 1163 (2022). § 73-59-17 requires the building official to refuse a permit absent evidence of licensure or exemption. § 73-59-3(1)(d), added by HB 1163 (2022) effective July 1, 2022, requires MSBOC licensing of electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and HVAC subcontractors of any tier on residential projects no matter the dollar amount — there is no $10,000 trade threshold; that figure is the residential remodeler definition at § 73-59-1(c), and the $50,000 figure is the residential builder definition at § 73-59-1(b). § 73-59-3(1)(c) with § 73-59-1(h) reaches a paid construction manager while excluding the owner. § 73-59-9 sets the criminal penalty ($100-$5,000 and/or 30-60 days) and § 73-59-9(2) bars an unlicensed person from enforcing the contract, limiting recovery to actual documented expenses proven by clear and convincing evidence; § 85-7-403(5) denies a lien to unlicensed contractors and to those who contracted with one.*

*Codes and permitting. There is no statewide residential code edition. SB 2378 (2014) § 1(1) requires a jurisdiction enforcing a building code to adopt one of the last three published editions, with residential fire-sprinkler provisions excluded by § 1(1)(b); § 1(3) provided a one-time 120-day opt-out that closed November 29-30, 2014, and subsequent building-code bills died in committee in 2017, 2021, and 2023. HB 1406 (2006) § 1(4) gave each of the five coastal counties (Jackson, Harrison, Hancock, Stone, Pearl River) and each municipality within them a 60-day opt-out; 2007's SB 2853, which would have added George County, died in committee. No official roster of opt-outs was published in either cycle. HB 1163 (2022) made permitting mandatory in every county and municipality via §§ 19-5-9(11) and 21-19-25(6); SB 2612 (2023), which would have allowed opting out, was vetoed. The Building Codes Council's authority is discretionary under § 17-2-3(5) and it has published no adopted code — verified by enumerating the Insurance Department's regulations index. The July 1, 2024 adoption was the State Fire Marshal's 2024 IFC and IBC, not the IRC and not for dwellings; the 2024 IRC adoption filed May 22, 2026 (19 Miss. Admin. Code Pt. 7, Ch. 3) covers modular homes only. Homeowner DIY restrictions are local ordinances under § 19-5-9 (counties, unincorporated areas only) or § 21-19-25 (municipalities), not state law.*

*Wastewater. § 41-67-5(1) requires a Notice of Intent before constructing the residence; § 41-67-5(2) bars a public water supply from connecting without prior written health-department approval, enforced by a $500 fine and water shutoff under § 41-67-28(5)(a); § 41-67-6(7) permits an affidavit exemption on lots of two acres or more subject to three conditions, with 22 counties overriding by ordinance.
