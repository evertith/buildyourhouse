# Kentucky Owner-Builder Permit Guide

*By a retired general contractor with 15+ years building custom homes — [about the author](/about). Last updated: August 2026.*

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Quick Answer: Can You Build Your Own House in Kentucky?">

**Yes — and in a good part of Kentucky you can do it without ever pulling a building permit.** Kentucky has no statewide general contractor license for residential work, so you can act as your own general contractor on a home you own.

The thing that trips people up here is that Kentucky separates the **code** from the **permit**. The Kentucky Residential Code (KRC) is a mandatory, uniform statewide standard — every new house in Kentucky is supposed to be built to it, everywhere. But building permits, inspections, and certificates of occupancy for houses are a **local option**: KRS 198B.060(1) says they "shall not be mandatory for single-family residences unless a local government passes an ordinance requiring inspections of single-family residences." The statute repeats the point at subsections (8) and (13), and the code regulation itself says the same thing at [815 KAR 7:125](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/kar/titles/815/007/125/) Section 2(2)(a): "Permits, inspections, and certificates of occupancy shall not be required for a single-family dwelling unless required by local ordinance."

Kentucky state-licenses electricians, plumbers, and HVAC contractors through the Department of Housing, Buildings and Construction (HBC), but statute lets a homeowner do their own electrical (KRS 227A.030(3)), plumbing (815 KAR 20:050), and HVAC (KRS 198B.674) work. And a few approvals apply in every county no matter what the local ordinance says: the state plumbing permit and its inspections, a certified electrical inspector's final certificate before you get permanent power, and the county health department's septic permit.

So the first question in Kentucky isn't "what does the permit cost" — it's "does a building permit exist where I'm building?" Ask your city **and** your county, because either one can adopt an ordinance.

Kentucky is one of the more interesting owner-builder states because it pairs **owner-builder freedom** (no state GC license, explicit homeowner trade exemptions) with **a single, predictable rulebook** (one mandatory statewide residential code) and then hands the decision about *enforcing* that rulebook on houses to each city and county. One standard, 120 counties' worth of answers about whether anyone will come look at your work.

That local option is not a technicality. On HBC's own county inspector sheets — 119 sheets covering the state, one for every county except Gallatin, tallied in August 2026 — 25 print "None" on the Local Building Inspector line, 6 carry the heading with nothing after it, and 10 have no such line at all. That's 41 of 119, roughly one county in three, where the sheet gives no local building inspector at all. Treat that as a signal, not a verdict: those sheets are a contact list, not a register of ordinances, and a city inside a county can require permits when the county doesn't. You still have to ask both.

The catch is that Kentucky sits on top of some of the most challenging ground in the eastern U.S.: vast karst (limestone) regions riddled with sinkholes, the New Madrid seismic zone in the far west, some of the highest radon levels in the country, and a brutal recent history of flash flooding. Those site hazards — not the permit office — are where Kentucky owner-builders get hurt.

## Kentucky Building Code Overview

<CalloutBox type="info" title="The Big Picture">

Kentucky operates a **mandatory uniform statewide code** with a **local-option permit**. Under KRS 198B.050 the state writes one residential code and it applies to every house in Kentucky. Under KRS 198B.060(1) the permit, the inspections, and the certificate of occupancy only exist where a local government has passed an ordinance requiring them. Those are two different questions, and mixing them up is the single most common mistake people make about building in Kentucky.

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="The 2023 NEC is in force, but not all of it">

Per HBC's own "July 15, 2026 NEC Update," three articles that had been held back on the 2017 NEC for one- and two-family dwellings — **210.52(C)**, **230.67**, and **314.27(C)** — became enforceable on **July 15, 2026**. Two things to keep straight:

- The **210.8(A)** and **210.8(D)(8), (9), (10) and (11)** GFCI requirements **remain delayed and are not yet enforceable**. Don't let anyone tell you the delay is over across the board; the GFCI piece is the part that's still parked.
- **Permits issued before July 15, 2026 are grandfathered** on the old provisions. If you pulled a permit in the spring of 2026, you are not retroactively on the new articles.
- HBC also clarified that **314.27(C)** reaches outlet boxes in **habitable rooms** — it "does not require every ceiling outlet box to be listed for ceiling fan support."

Wiring is the one area where Kentucky's dates genuinely move. Confirm the current enforcement notice at [dhbc.ky.gov](https://dhbc.ky.gov/) before you buy boxes and devices.

Two things make Kentucky unusual. First, the standard really is uniform. Under KRS 198B.050 and [815 KAR 7:125](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/kar/titles/815/007/125/), the 2015 IRC as amended is the mandatory state residential building code for single-family dwellings, two-family dwellings, and townhouses built in Kentucky. Your county does not get to write its own house code, water down the KRC, or bolt extra construction requirements onto it. What your county *does* get to decide is whether anyone checks — that's the permit ordinance under KRS 198B.060(1), and it's a separate question from the standard.

You'll see Kentucky described online as a "mini/maxi" state where local governments can adopt neither weaker nor stronger residential requirements. Be careful with that framing: it isn't in KRS 198B.050, .060, or .080, and KRS 198B.060(1) plainly lets a local government require permits the state does not. Where the statutes do preempt local add-ons, they say so narrowly and by subject — KRS 198B.6673(4), for instance, bars additional local HVAC requirements specifically, which would be pointless language if a blanket preemption already existed. The safe reading is the one the statutes support: **one mandatory statewide standard, local option on permits and inspections.**

Second, the residential energy code is genuinely old — Kentucky still references the **2009 IECC**, one of the more lenient residential energy codes still in force anywhere in the country.

The code applies everywhere. Whether anyone shows up to enforce it on your house depends entirely on where you build:

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="There is no state backstop for houses">

This is the part people get wrong most often, so be clear about it: if your county has no building-permit ordinance, the state does **not** step in and inspect your house. KRS 198B.060(4)(b) says the Department "shall not preempt or assert jurisdiction for the enforcement of the code on single-family dwellings." HBC's own county sheets say the same thing in plainer words where no local inspector exists — "None / For Commercial construction Contact Dept. Housing, Buildings Construction for building permits." Commercial only. Nobody at the state level is coming to look at your footings.

Cut both ways with that. It means a rural Kentucky build can be far less bureaucratic than a Kentucky metro build. It also means the code compliance of your house is on your conscience and, if it ever goes wrong, in a courtroom rather than a plan-review comment.

</CalloutBox>

### The Approvals That Apply No Matter Where You Build

Even in a county with no building permit at all, four things still apply to a new house:

1. **The state plumbing permit.** Plumbing is not a local matter in Kentucky. KRS 318.134(1)(a) puts permit issuance with the Department, and KRS 318.015(1) applies the plumbing chapter in all counties. You (or your plumber) get a state plumbing permit and state plumbing inspections wherever you build.
2. **The state HVAC permit.** KRS 198B.6671(1) requires a permit before an initial heating, ventilation, or air conditioning system is installed.
3. **The electrical certificate.** Your wiring is signed off by a **certified electrical inspector**, not by a building department, and that certificate is what unlocks power (below).
4. **The septic permit**, from the county health department, if you're not on a sewer.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="The two chokepoints: power and water">

If you're tempted to think a no-ordinance county means nobody can stop you, these are the two places Kentucky can and does stop you — because utilities are barred from connecting you:

- **No permanent electrical service** until a certified electrical inspector issues the final certificate of approval (KRS 198B.060(11)).
- **No permanent water** until the plumbing has been approved (KRS 318.165).

Plan the electrical inspector and the state plumbing permit into your schedule from day one. In a no-permit county these are the only inspections you'll get — and they're also the ones standing between you and a house you can actually live in.

</CalloutBox>

### Kentucky-Specific Amendments

The KRC modifies the base 2015 IRC in several areas that matter to an owner-builder. One caveat first: the Kentucky Residential Code is a copyrighted code book, not free text on a state website, so the specifics below are the figures builders and inspectors commonly cite rather than something you can pull up and verify online. Confirm each one in the code book or with your inspector before you build to it.

1. **Frost depth**: commonly cited as 24 inches across most of the state, rising toward 30–33 inches in the eastern mountains and Northern Kentucky (KRC Table R403.1.4). Get the number for your county from your inspector or the code book before you dig footings — this is not a figure to take from any website, including this one
2. **Seismic design**: most of Kentucky is Seismic Design Category A or B, but the far-western Jackson Purchase counties are commonly assigned SDC D0, D1, or even D2 because of the New Madrid seismic zone (KRC R301.2.2). Confirm your county's category before designing foundations and bracing
3. **Energy efficiency**: uses the 2009 IECC, notably less stringent than Ohio's 2018 IECC or Virginia's newer code
4. **Radon**: radon-resistant construction is widely understood not to be a statewide requirement — the IRC's radon appendix is treated as optional. Confirm with your inspector, and see the radon section below for why you should build the rough-in regardless
5. **Sprinklers**: the IRC fire-sprinkler mandate for one- and two-family dwellings is generally understood not to have been adopted in Kentucky. Confirm locally if it affects your design

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="No statewide radon mandate — despite the radon risk">

Kentucky is generally understood not to require radon-resistant construction in new houses, even though the state has one of the highest average indoor radon readings in the country (about 9.45 pCi/L, well above the 4.0 pCi/L action level). Verify the current adoption with your inspector — but either way the radon rough-in is your decision, not the code's. Build it in anyway; see the radon section below.

</CalloutBox>

## Kentucky Owner-Builder Laws

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Where the freedom comes from">

Kentucky has no statewide general contractor licensing law for residential building. That's the foundation of owner-builder freedom here.

</CalloutBox>

The Kentucky Department of Housing, Buildings and Construction ([HBC, at dhbc.ky.gov](https://dhbc.ky.gov/)) licenses the specialty trades — electrical, plumbing, and HVAC contractors — plus boilers, elevators, manufactured housing, and sprinkler systems. It does **not** license general contractors. Where GC registration exists, it's local: Louisville, Lexington, and Bowling Green each run their own contractor registration programs.

Two things on the HBC site are worth your time before you do anything else. **"Find Currently Enforced Code"** tells you which editions are actually in force right now, which matters in Kentucky because the electrical dates move and the Department's own fuel gas references don't always agree. **"Contact an Inspector"** gives you the sheet for your county — it names your plumbing inspector and your electrical inspector, points you to the septic contact, and tells you whether a local building inspector exists at all. Start there.

### Legal Rights

You may act as your own general contractor on your own property because:

- Kentucky does not require a state-issued general contractor license (residential or otherwise)
- Where a building permit exists, cities and counties allow homeowners to pull their own as owner-builder
- Kentucky statute gives homeowners explicit exemptions to perform their own electrical, plumbing, and HVAC work (detailed below)

### Critical Restrictions and Requirements

**Local Permit Requirements**: Where a building permit ordinance exists, the permit comes from a local office. Most building departments require:

- Proof of ownership (deed or title)
- A statement that the home will be your primary residence
- For the trades, a signed homeowner affidavit (see below)
- Plans showing compliance with the KRC
- The workers' compensation and unemployment insurance affidavit described below

**The affidavit every Kentucky permit needs**: under KRS 198B.060(10), no building department in Kentucky may issue a permit until the applicant files an affidavit about workers' compensation and unemployment insurance coverage. Owner-builders skate past this one without reading it, and the penalty clause is not small: a false affidavit exposes you to a penalty "not to exceed four thousand dollars ($4,000) or an amount equal to the sum of all uninsured and unsatisfied claims … whichever is greater." If you're paying anyone to swing a hammer on your job, understand your workers' comp position before you sign that form, not after somebody gets hurt.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Skipping the certificate of occupancy has a ten-year tail">

If you build in a county with no permit ordinance, there's no certificate of occupancy to get — but understand what that costs you later. KRS 198B.130 lets someone bringing a code-violation claim recover reasonable attorney's fees on top of damages where **no certificate of occupancy was issued**, and the window runs up to **ten years after the building was first occupied**. Selling an uninspected owner-built house is legal in much of Kentucky. It also means a buyer with a code complaint has a decade and a fee-shifting statute on their side. Document your build — photos of every rough-in, receipts, inspection paperwork from the trades that did get inspected — as if you'll need to prove compliance in year nine, because you might.

</CalloutBox>

They are not, however, the same rule three times. The electrical exemption is broad; the plumbing and HVAC exemptions are permits with conditions attached, and their five-year limits are written differently:

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Read the two five-year rules carefully — they are not the same rule">

People repeat this as "one homeowner permit every five years for plumbing and HVAC." That's wrong in a way that can cost you.

- **Plumbing** (815 KAR 20:050 §2(1)(b)5) only bars another homeowner permit **for construction of a new home** inside five years.
- **HVAC** (815 KAR 8:070 §2(3)) bars **any** second homeowner HVAC construction permit inside five years — no new-home qualifier. It's the tighter of the two.

One more wrinkle worth knowing on HVAC: the statutory exemption at KRS 198B.674(3) is narrower than the regulation that implements it, reaching property that is **owned *and* occupied**. If you're installing HVAC in a house you own but haven't moved into yet, ask HBC how they read that before you file — don't assume the regulation's friendlier wording governs.

Across all three, the constant is this: the exemption is from the **license**, never from the **inspection**. Gas-line work is generally outside the homeowner plumbing exemption; verify before touching gas.

</CalloutBox>

**The HVAC inspection clock**: once your HVAC work is ready, KRS 198B.6672(3) sets a statutory timeframe for the inspection to happen. If you're waiting past it, you have a statute to point at — useful when you're trying to close in a house and the schedule has gone quiet.

### Farm Exemptions

Two carve-outs matter if you're building on agricultural land:

- **Plumbing**: KRS 318.015(3) carves farmsteads out of the state plumbing chapter. This is a real exemption from a requirement that otherwise applies in all 120 counties — worth confirming your situation qualifies, because plumbing is one of the few approvals that normally follows you everywhere.
- **Farm buildings in urban-county governments**: KRS 198B.060(3) exempts farm buildings from permit requirements in urban-county government territory. Note the limit — this is about farm buildings, not the farmhouse.

### Liability and Insurance

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="As owner-builder, the liability is yours">

As an owner-builder in Kentucky:

- You're personally liable for any injuries on-site (workers' comp recommended for paid labor)
- You can typically obtain builder's risk insurance, but rates are higher than for licensed contractors
- Some lenders require owner-builders to carry liability insurance during construction
- Kentucky has seller disclosure requirements that follow the home after sale

</CalloutBox>

### Seller Disclosure

[Kentucky Revised Statutes § 324.360](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/statutes/statute.aspx?id=31615) requires sellers of single-family residential property to complete the Kentucky Real Estate Commission's "Seller's Disclosure of Property Condition" (KREC Form 402) when a licensee markets the property — covering basement and roof leaks, water and sewage source and condition, and the working condition of component systems. Owner-built homes don't have to be labeled as such, but known defects, unpermitted work, or code issues must be disclosed. (New homes sold with a warranty, auctions, and foreclosures are exempt from the form.)

## Permit Costs in Kentucky

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Every local figure below is illustrative — verify before budgeting">

The local fee figures in this section are illustrative planning numbers compiled from published fee schedules, not quotes. Fee schedules change, jurisdictions restructure them, and site conditions move the total more than the permit does. Call your building department and your utility for current numbers before you budget. The one number here that comes from a regulation rather than a fee schedule is the state plumbing permit, below.

</CalloutBox>

Kentucky permit costs are low by national standards — the *building* permit itself is cheap almost everywhere, and in a county with no permit ordinance it's zero. The real money is in utility tap/connection fees and, on tough sites, geotechnical work.

### The State Plumbing Permit

This one applies wherever you build and its fee is set by regulation, not by a county. [815 KAR 20:050](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/kar/titles/815/020/050/) Section 4 puts the state plumbing permit at **$50 base plus $14 per fixture**, with five inspections included in that fee. For a typical house with 12–15 fixtures that's roughly $220–$260. Budget it separately from anything your county charges, and don't expect your local building department to handle it — the permit comes from the Department under KRS 318.134(1)(a).

One reading caution if you go look the fee up yourself: Kentucky publishes amended regulations as new text followed by old text with **no strikethrough**, so a current fee can appear on the page as something like "fifty (50) forty-five (45) dollars." The **first** number is the current one.

## Inspection Requirements

Kentucky does not prescribe a statutory residential inspection sequence. There's no state list of 13 inspections your house must pass — what you get depends on whether your jurisdiction has a permit program and how that program runs its schedule. Split the question in two.

### What Happens Everywhere

These don't depend on a local ordinance:

- **State plumbing inspections.** The state plumbing permit fee covers five inspections (815 KAR 20:050 §4), and no permanent water connection comes before plumbing approval (KRS 318.165).
- **The electrical certificate.** A certified electrical inspector inspects your wiring and issues the final certificate of approval; without it you don't get permanent power (KRS 198B.060(11)).
- **HVAC inspection** on the state HVAC permit, with a statutory timeframe at KRS 198B.6672(3).
- **Septic**, through the county health department, if you're not on sewer.

Your county inspector sheet at [dhbc.ky.gov](https://dhbc.ky.gov/) names the plumbing and electrical inspectors for your county. Those are the calls you'll be making regardless of where you build.

### What Happens Only Where There's a Local Program

Footings, foundation, framing, insulation, and the final building inspection with a certificate of occupancy exist **only** if your city or county adopted a permit ordinance. In a no-ordinance county, none of the inspections below happen — the KRC still applies to your house, but nobody is coming to verify it.

## Karst, Sinkholes, Seismic & Flooding: Kentucky's Hazard Map

This is the section that matters most in Kentucky, and the one most owner-builders underestimate. The permit office is easy here. The *ground* is not.

### Karst Topography and Sinkholes (Central and South-Central Kentucky)

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="A huge share of Kentucky sits on cave-riddled limestone">

Much of central and south-central Kentucky — the Pennyroyal (Pennyrile) sinkhole plain around Bowling Green, Glasgow, and the Mammoth Cave region, plus the Bluegrass around Lexington — is **karst**: soluble limestone bedrock honeycombed with caves, voids, and sinkholes. Build the wrong spot and the ground can literally open under the house.

</CalloutBox>

The risk is not theoretical. On February 12, 2014, a sinkhole 40 feet wide and 30 feet deep opened beneath the National Corvette Museum's Skydome in Bowling Green and swallowed eight Corvettes — a direct result of the region's karst geology. The Pennyroyal sinkhole plain has one of the densest concentrations of sinkholes in the United States.

What this means for an owner-builder:

- **Get a geotechnical evaluation in karst areas.** Borings, and on higher-risk sites ground-penetrating radar or microgravity surveys, can find voids before you build over them. Preventive grouting is a fraction of the cost of emergency sinkhole repair.
- **Manage drainage deliberately.** Concentrated stormwater (downspouts, driveways) accelerates sinkhole formation; route it away and avoid dumping water into existing sinks.
- **Standard homeowner insurance usually excludes sinkhole/earth-movement loss in Kentucky** — confirm coverage and consider a rider.
- **Foundations** in karst may need to bridge or span suspect zones, or bear on competent rock confirmed by borings.

### New Madrid Seismic Zone (Far-Western Kentucky)

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="The far-western Purchase region is real earthquake country">

The Jackson Purchase counties in far-western Kentucky — around Paducah, Mayfield, and Fulton — sit near the New Madrid Seismic Zone, and the KRC puts them in the highest seismic design categories in the state.

</CalloutBox>

Seismic design categories are assigned by county in KRC R301.2.2, which lives in the copyrighted code book rather than on a public state page. The county-by-county assignments commonly cited put the far-western Purchase counties (Ballard, Carlisle, Fulton, Graves, Hickman, McCracken and their neighbors) at SDC D2, with a ring of D1 and D0 counties around them — Calloway, Livingston, Marshall, then Caldwell, Crittenden, Lyon, Trigg, Union. Most of central and eastern Kentucky is SDC A or B with no special seismic detailing. **Confirm your county's actual category in the code book or with your inspector before designing anything**, because the difference between B and D2 is the difference between conventional framing and an engineered lateral system.

If you build anywhere in the Purchase region, expect SDC D detailing in your foundations, wall bracing, and connections: more rebar, engineered hold-downs, braced-wall design, and an engineer on your team. This is one place where the local-option permit rule doesn't save you any work — an uninspected house in a D2 county is still a house in a D2 county.

### Flooding (Ohio River Valley and Eastern Kentucky)

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Kentucky floods — and not only inside the mapped floodplain">

Kentucky has serious riverine and flash-flood exposure: the Ohio River corridor in the north, and the steep Appalachian hollows of eastern Kentucky.

</CalloutBox>

In late July 2022, catastrophic flash flooding hit eastern Kentucky: 44 people died, 13 counties were declared federal disaster areas, and more than 8,400 homes were damaged with hundreds destroyed — many of them **outside officially mapped FEMA floodplains**. The lesson for owner-builders is blunt: the flood maps are a floor, not a guarantee.

Practical steps:

- **Check the FEMA flood map for your parcel**, and then look higher — at local topography, drainage, and flood history. In steep eastern Kentucky, build well above the hollow floor.
- **If any part of your lot is in a Special Flood Hazard Area**, you'll need a floodplain development permit and likely lowest-floor elevation above base flood elevation.
- **Along the Ohio River** (Louisville, Northern Kentucky, Owensboro, Henderson), riverine flooding and required elevation/floodproofing can drive design and cost.

### Radon (Statewide, Worst in Karst)

Kentucky has some of the highest indoor radon levels in the country — a statewide average near 9.45 pCi/L, more than double the 4.0 pCi/L action level, with 30 of 120 counties in EPA Radon Zone 1. The same karst limestone that produces sinkholes also produces radon. Yet radon-resistant construction is generally understood not to be required in Kentucky houses, and nothing stops you from building it. Do it:

- Vapor barrier under slab
- 4" gas-permeable layer (gravel) under slab
- 3" or 4" vent pipe routed from sub-slab to roof
- Electrical outlet near the pipe for a future fan
- Labeling at penetrations

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Don't skip the radon rough-in">

This adds roughly $400-$900 during construction and is dramatically cheaper than retrofitting later. With Kentucky's radon levels, future buyers (and your own lungs) will care.

</CalloutBox>

## Common Questions

**Do I need a license to build my own house in Kentucky?** No. Kentucky does not require a state general contractor license, so building your own home as owner-builder is allowed. Whether you need a building permit depends on your city and county — under KRS 198B.060(1) permits are a local option for single-family homes — but the home is supposed to meet the mandatory statewide Kentucky Residential Code either way. If you hire out the trades, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC contractors are state-licensed by HBC; you can also do those trades yourself under the statutory homeowner exemptions.

**Can you build your own house without a permit in Kentucky?** In much of Kentucky, yes. KRS 198B.060(1) says permits, inspections, and certificates of occupancy "shall not be mandatory for single-family residences unless a local government passes an ordinance requiring inspections of single-family residences," and 815 KAR 7:125 Section 2(2)(a) repeats it. Where no ordinance exists there's no building permit to pull and no state substitute — KRS 198B.060(4)(b) bars the Department from asserting jurisdiction over single-family dwellings. What doesn't change: the Kentucky Residential Code still applies to the house, and you still need the state plumbing permit, the certified electrical inspector's certificate, the state HVAC permit, and a septic permit if you're not on sewer. Ask both your city and your county before assuming anything.

**What is the Kentucky owner-builder exemption?** Kentucky has no state general contractor license to be exempt from, so "owner-builder" just means you act as your own GC and pull whatever permits apply where you're building. Separately, statute gives homeowners specific exemptions to do their own electrical (KRS 227A.030(3)), plumbing (815 KAR 20:050), and HVAC (KRS 198B.674) work.

**How much does a Kentucky owner-builder permit cost?** Where a local building permit exists it's cheap — illustrative figures for a 2,000 sq ft home run about $200-$400 — and in a county with no permit ordinance there's no building permit fee at all. The state plumbing permit applies everywhere at $50 plus $14 per fixture (815 KAR 20:050 §4). Total permit-related cost is driven by utility tap and capacity fees: roughly $5,000-$13,000 in the metros, often less (but with well and septic instead) in rural areas. Verify all local figures before budgeting.

**Which Kentucky counties are best for owner-builders?** Boone County/Northern Kentucky and Daviess/Owensboro offer organized permitting with manageable costs; Warren/Bowling Green and Fayette/Lexington offer strong markets if you do proper karst due diligence. Avoid building in the far-western Purchase region or a karst hot spot without an engineer.

## Final Thoughts for Kentucky Owner-Builders

Kentucky is a genuinely good owner-builder state with one big asterisk. The good part: no state general contractor license, explicit statutory rights to do your own electrical, plumbing, and HVAC, a lenient 2009-IECC energy code, one predictable statewide rulebook so you're never guessing which standard applies, and — in a large share of the state — no building permit at all. The asterisk: Kentucky's *ground* is some of the trickiest in the eastern U.S.

The big decisions:

1. **Settle the permit question first.** Call your city and your county and ask whether either has an ordinance requiring permits and inspections for single-family homes. That one answer determines whether you're running a plan-review process or building on your own judgment. Start with your county's inspector sheet at [dhbc.ky.gov](https://dhbc.ky.gov/), then confirm by phone — the sheet is a contact list, not a register of ordinances.
2. **Vet the site before the house.** In central and south-central Kentucky karst, a geotechnical investigation isn't optional — ask the Corvette Museum. In the far west, expect New Madrid seismic detailing. In the east, build above the flash-flood line even when the FEMA map says you're clear.
3. **Use your homeowner trade exemptions, but read the two five-year rules separately.** Plumbing bars another homeowner permit for a new home within five years; HVAC bars any second homeowner permit within five years. And the electrical exemption is broader than most people think — KRS 227A.030(3) covers a homeowner or farmer wiring their own real property, with no residency test attached.
4. **Build the radon rough-in.** Kentucky doesn't require it, but Kentucky has among the worst radon in the country. Do it during construction.
5. **If nobody is inspecting, inspect yourself.** A no-ordinance county is freedom with a bill attached: the KRC still applies, KRS 198B.130 gives a future claimant attorney's fees and a ten-year window where no certificate of occupancy issued, and you have no inspection record to point at. Hire a private third-party inspector for footings, framing, and pre-drywall, and photograph everything. It's a few hundred dollars against a decade of exposure.
6. **Get an engineer where the site demands it** — karst, SDC D seismic, steep eastern slopes, or river floodplain. It's the cheapest insurance you'll buy.

Kentucky rewards the owner-builder who respects the dirt. Get the site right, know which approvals actually apply to you, and the rest of the process is about as owner-builder-friendly as the eastern U.S. gets.
