# Louisiana Owner-Builder Permit Guide

*By a retired general contractor with 15+ years building custom homes — [about the author](/about). Last updated: September 2026.*

<CalloutBox type="info" title="Quick Answer: Can You Build Your Own House in Louisiana?">

**Yes — and there's a specific statute that says so.** Louisiana requires a state residential contractor license once a project's value reaches $50,000, but an owner building their **own personal residence is expressly exempt** under La. R.S. 37:2157(A)(13) — you file a notarized "affidavit claiming exemption from licensure" with the permit office and act as your own contractor. Two catches: you can't build more than one residence per year, with the year running from the date your **certificate of occupancy issues**, and the home must be **yours to live in**. Every home in the state must also meet the mandatory [Louisiana State Uniform Construction Code (LSUCC)](https://lsuccc.la/codes-standards/) — the 2021 International Residential Code with strong post-Katrina wind and flood amendments — enforced by your parish or municipal building department. Two things to know before you read any other Louisiana guide: the exemption statute nearly everyone still cites, R.S. 37:2170, was **repealed in 2022**, and the entire construction-code chapter **moved to a different Title of the Revised Statutes on 1 August 2026**. Confirm permit and trade rules with your specific parish.

</CalloutBox>

Louisiana is a genuinely interesting owner-builder state, but for the opposite reason Ohio is. Ohio is friendly because it barely regulates. Louisiana regulates hard — it has one of the strongest statewide codes in the country — yet it pairs that with a clean, statutory owner-builder exemption. You give up the "wild west" freedom of unregulated rural counties, but you gain a clear legal right to build your own home and a code that, frankly, exists to keep your house standing through the next hurricane.

That code is the [Louisiana State Uniform Construction Code (LSUCC)](https://lsuccc.la/codes-standards/), created after Hurricanes Katrina and Rita by Act 12 of the 2005 First Extraordinary Session and now mandated statewide by La. R.S. 37:3737(A)(1). It is administered by the **Louisiana Uniform Construction Code Commission (LUCCC)**, and — this is the key difference from Ohio — **every parish and municipality must enforce it**. There is no "no-code parish" in Louisiana.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="The statute numbers changed on 1 August 2026, and most sources have not caught up">

If you have already read a few Louisiana permit guides, you have probably seen the construction code cited to **La. R.S. 40:1730.21 through 1730.40.2**. Those sections were **repealed in their entirety** by Acts 2026, No. 881 (HB 1186), which re-enacted the whole program as Chapter 62 of Title 37 — **La. R.S. 37:3727 through 3750** — effective **1 August 2026**. The same act renamed the agency: the Louisiana State Uniform Construction Code **Council (LSUCCC)** is now the Louisiana Uniform Construction Code **Commission (LUCCC)**.

Nothing about what you may build changed. What changed is which citation is live — and that matters the day you are standing at a permit counter quoting law at a clerk. Expect to meet the old numbers on parish handouts, code-adoption trackers and competing guides for a long while yet.

</CalloutBox>

## Louisiana Building Code Overview

<CalloutBox type="info" title="The Big Picture">

Louisiana operates a **mandatory statewide code with local enforcement**. The state (through the LUCCC) adopts the code; every parish and municipality is required by law to enforce it. Unlike Ohio or Texas, there is no rural area where the residential code simply doesn't apply.

</CalloutBox>

The Commission reviews and updates the code on a multi-year cycle. The current package is the **2021 I-Codes with state amendments**, and the **2024 I-Codes and 2023 NEC are scheduled to take effect January 1, 2027**. Confirm the live edition with your parish before you draw plans — and read the next section before you decide when to file.

### The Filing Date Locks Your Code Edition

This is the most useful sentence in Louisiana law for anyone building in 2026 or 2027, and it appears in almost no guide. La. R.S. 37:3734:

> "Notwithstanding any other law to the contrary, a licensed inspector shall conduct a building inspection using the requirements of the codes in effect for the locality on the date of the application for the original building permit."

Your house is judged against the codes in force **on the day you applied**, not the day the inspector shows up. With the 2024 I-Codes and 2023 NEC arriving January 1, 2027, an application filed before that line holds your build to the 2021 I-Codes and the 2020 NEC — through a framing inspection that might not happen until 2028, and a final that might not happen until 2029.

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="The date you file is a design decision">

If you are close to permit-ready in late 2026, work out what the 2024 I-Codes and 2023 NEC would cost you before you decide to file in the new year. The 2023 NEC alone carries changes worth pricing. This provision is new with Act 881 — it has no counterpart in the repealed Title 40 sections — so don't expect your parish counter to volunteer it.

</CalloutBox>

### Statewide Enforcement — No "No-Code" Parishes

This is the single most important structural fact about building in Louisiana, and it's the inverse of Ohio. After Katrina, the legislature made the LSUCC the **minimum** code everywhere. A parish or municipality may enforce it more strictly (and many coastal ones do), but none may opt out of one- and two-family dwelling requirements the way an unregulated rural Ohio or Texas county effectively can. La. R.S. 37:3737(A)(1) says all municipalities and parishes "shall enforce" the Uniform Construction Code and adds that "nothing in this Chapter allows any local government to avoid enforcement." The older mechanism by which a parish could file an affidavit claiming exemption is gone from the operative text.

One thing not to count on: the **State Fire Marshal is not a statewide residential fallback**. Its standing jurisdiction expressly excludes one- and two-family dwellings under La. R.S. 37:3735(C), and it enforces on a parish's behalf only by agreement. Where a parish is thinly staffed, the answer is the private-inspector route below, not the fire marshal.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Don't assume a rural Louisiana parish is unregulated">

Unlike Ohio's certified-department patchwork, Louisiana's code is mandatory statewide. Even lightly staffed rural parishes enforce the LSUCC minimum (often through a third-party plan-review provider). Confirm who issues permits in your parish before assuming you can build without inspection — and note that every parish government in the state holds an active code-enforcement credential with the Commission. There is no such thing as a no-permit parish here.

</CalloutBox>

#### If your parish counter is quiet, the statute names your route

This is the part most guides leave out. A thinly staffed parish is a scheduling problem, not a permission slip — and La. R.S. 37:3737(A)(1) gives owner-builders a named remedy: "licensed contractors, and **homeowners exempted from the contractor licensing law pursuant to R.S. 37:2157**, may establish agreements with private inspectors to conduct plan reviews, inspections, and enforce the State Uniform Construction Code."

One condition, and it is the one that bites: the parish will not accept a private inspector's report unless that inspector **already has a contract with, or is registered with, your jurisdiction**. So the call to make is to the parish, not the inspector — ask who is on their list before you hire anyone.

### Two Things No Parish May Require

Both of these are money, and both are worth knowing before a plan reviewer asks you for something they cannot actually demand.

**No architect or engineer stamp on a prescriptive IRC house.** La. R.S. 37:3737(D): "a municipality or parish shall not require that a residential building plan for a one- or two-family dwelling be prepared or stamped by a licensed architect or engineer if that dwelling falls within the prescriptive standards of the International Residential Code currently adopted in the Uniform Construction Code." Step outside the prescriptive tables — long spans, tall walls, an engineered foundation, most of what south Louisiana soils force on you — and you will need the engineer anyway. But a conventional IRC house does not need a stamp just because the counter would prefer one.

**No residential fire sprinklers, and no local ordinance may add them.** La. R.S. 37:3733(A)(3) bars the Commission from adopting any sprinkler requirement for one- and two-family dwellings and, separately, bars a municipality or parish from adopting or enforcing an ordinance requiring one.

### Louisiana-Specific Amendments

The LSUCC modifies the base IRC in several safety-critical areas — almost all aimed at wind and water:

1. **Hurricane wind design**: Coastal and south Louisiana are designed for very high wind speeds (roughly 130–160 mph depending on parish and distance from the coast), with wind-borne debris protection required near the coast
2. **Flood elevation**: Louisiana writes no freeboard *number* into its code, but it does set a **statewide floor by reference to ASCE 24-14** that no parish may go under — and parishes may go above it. This is the single most misreported rule in the state; see the flood section below for the amendment text
3. **Energy efficiency**: Uses the 2021 IECC; nearly the entire state is the warm-humid Climate Zone 2A, so the emphasis is on cooling, humidity control and air sealing rather than heavy insulation
4. **Termite protection**: Soil pretreatment for subterranean (including Formosan) termites is effectively required on new construction — see the hazard section
5. **Electrical**: Uses the 2020 NEC directly rather than the IRC's electrical chapters — confirm the edition your parish is on before wiring

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="The code is your friend on the coast">

In a state where homes routinely face 130+ mph winds and Gulf storm surge, the LSUCC's wind and flood amendments aren't bureaucratic box-checking — they're the difference between a house that survives a hurricane and one that doesn't. Build to them generously.

</CalloutBox>

## Louisiana Owner-Builder Laws

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="The statute everyone cites for this was repealed in 2022">

Nearly every Louisiana owner-builder page in circulation — and this guide, before this revision — cites **La. R.S. 37:2170** for the exemption. That section, and R.S. 37:2171 with it, were **repealed by Acts 2022, No. 195, §2**. The owner-builder exemption now lives at **La. R.S. 37:2157(A)(13)**.

The threshold in the same circulation, **$75,000**, is also stale. It is **$50,000** now. Louisiana rewrote this chapter three times in four years — Acts 2022 No. 195, Acts 2024 No. 178 and Acts 2025 No. 422.

Neither correction changes what you may do. Both change whether you can prove it at the counter.

</CalloutBox>

The [Louisiana State Licensing Board for Contractors (LSLBC)](https://lslbc.gov/types-of-licenses/) licenses general, residential, electrical and mechanical (HVAC) contractors — note that it licenses the **business**, not the individual tradesman. A **residential contractor license is required when project value reaches $50,000 or more**, per the definitions at La. R.S. 37:2150.1(19)(a) and 37:2150.1(4)(a)(ii). Home-improvement work on an existing home from **$7,500 up to $50,000** needs a Home Improvement registration; commercial work is licensed at $50,000.

**How Louisiana measures the number is broader than people expect**, and this is where readers talk themselves into trouble. La. R.S. 37:2150.1(3): project value "includes the entire cost of the labor, materials, rentals, and all direct and indirect project expenses. The cost of materials, rentals, and direct and indirect expenses **shall be included regardless of who pays the costs or if they are donated**." Buying your own materials, or having a brother-in-law donate the lumber, does not get you under the line. (The statute assigns no dollar value to your own uncompensated labor, and neither will I.)

### Legal Rights

You may act as your own contractor on your own property because La. R.S. 37:2157(A)(13) exempts:

- Owners who "supervise, superintend, oversee, direct, or in any manner assume charge" of constructing **their personal residences**
- Provided the owner does not build more than **one residence per year**, with the year running "from the date of issuance of the certificate of occupancy"
- You file a notarized **affidavit claiming exemption from licensure** (on a form provided by the LSLBC) to obtain the building permit

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Read the verbs — Louisiana's exemption is broader than most states'">

"Supervise, superintend, oversee, direct, or in any manner assume charge" are **management** verbs. You may act as your own general contractor and hire licensed subs; you do not have to swing the hammer yourself. The proof is in the same statute: R.S. 37:2157(A)(15)(a) exempts an owner who "**physically performs**" home improvement work — and those words are deliberately absent from (13).

The flip side, and take this as the safe reading rather than settled law: your subs almost certainly do not ride on your exemption. R.S. 37:2157(A)(17) exempts subcontractors only when they work "for a residential construction license holder" — and an exempt owner is not a license holder — and it carves out electrical, mechanical and plumbing regardless. No case or board ruling construing that paragraph against an exempt owner turned up. Hire licensed trades and don't be the test case.

</CalloutBox>

### Critical Restrictions and Requirements

**It must be your residence, not a spec house.** The operative words in (A)(13) are "their personal residences." There is no stated holding period and no anti-resale clause in this exemption — the "will not be for sale or rent" language people quote belongs to a *different* exemption at §2157(A)(2), which also demands controlled access and is unusable on a house lot. Don't let the two be conflated. But building to sell still puts you outside (13) entirely, and selling shortly after completion draws scrutiny, so keep records showing you intended to live there.

**One home per year.** R.S. 37:2157(A)(13) limits the exemption to one personal residence in a one-year period, and the statute is specific about when the clock starts: "the one-year period shall commence on the date of issuance of the certificate of occupancy." That is not the same event as moving in, and on a self-built house the two can be months apart. There are narrow exceptions: you may build an additional residence within the year if your **legal marital status changes**, or if a **change in employment requires you to relocate more than 50 miles** from your personal residence.

**The affidavit — this is mandatory, not a formality.** The document is the LSLBC's "**Affidavit Claiming Exemption from Licensure**": notarized, carrying no form number but a revision date, and structured as eleven separate statements you initial one at a time. The form comes from the state board; the signed affidavit goes to your **local parish or municipal permit official**, not back to the board. R.S. 37:2160(C) requires it "prior to the issuance of a permit."

The reason your clerk will not budge on it is R.S. 37:2160(B), which forbids a local building department from issuing a permit to an unlicensed person at all, except for work requiring no license. The affidavit is how you prove you are in that carve-out — it is the door, not the paperwork.

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Grab the right form — there are two on the same page">

The LSLBC's [forms page](https://lslbc.gov/forms/) also carries a **Church Owner/Builder Affidavit**, which implements a completely different exemption. Confusingly, the words "Owner/Builder" appear in the *church* form's title and not in yours. The one you want is the "Affidavit Claiming Exemption from Licensure."

</CalloutBox>

**Homeowner doing their own trade work.** Louisiana's answer is different for each trade, and it is worth being precise, because the hedged version of this you'll read elsewhere makes readers doubt a right they actually have.

**Plumbing — yes, and the mechanism is stronger than an exemption.** There is no homeowner carve-out in the plumbing *licensing* sections; R.S. 37:1367 requires the board's license of any natural person doing the work and names no owner. The allowance sits in the **definitions** instead: R.S. 37:1377(D) provides that for purposes of the chapter, "plumbing" does not include "**(8) Work done by an individual on his own personal residence**." Because it is definitional, the licensing requirement never attaches to you in the first place. One neighboring paragraph is worth knowing about: (D)(9), a separate owner exclusion for *maintenance* work, expressly "shall not include construction or installation" — a limit paragraph (8) does not carry.

**Gas fitting — a separate, express exemption.** R.S. 37:1367(J)(2): "the provisions of this Subsection shall not apply to work performed by persons on their own residences." It attaches only to the gas-fitter subsection, so cite it by itself.

**Electrical — the state license you are worried about does not exist.** Louisiana has no journeyman or master electrician license and no state board of electrical examiners. The LSLBC licenses the electrical **contractor**, meaning the business selling the work. Credentialing individuals is left to municipalities under R.S. 33:4782, which says municipalities "**may** enact" such ordinances and "**may in their discretion**" empower a board — permissive, not mandatory, with New Orleans expressly excluded from the whole section. What this means practically: there is no state license standing between you and your own wiring, but whether a homeowner may pull the electrical permit is a **local** question. Ask your permit office, in those words, before you buy wire.

In every case the work is inspected to the same LSUCC standard as a licensed contractor's.

<CalloutBox type="tip" title="Three constraints on doing your own trade work">

It must be your own personal residence, you must pull the permit yourself, and the work is held to the same code standard as a licensed pro's. The plumbing allowance is the firmest of the three because it is written into the definition of "plumbing" rather than granted as an exemption — but confirm with your specific parish who may pull the electrical and mechanical permits before you start.

</CalloutBox>

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="One quirk: the plumbing chapter's reach is geographic">

R.S. 37:1375 applies the plumbing chapter to "all cities, towns, villages, communities and public sewerage and/or water districts," plus everything within one mile of those boundaries or facilities. The section was last amended in the 1960s and **"communities" is nowhere defined**. Plenty of rural Louisiana parcels sit in a genuine grey area. Don't decide on your own that your lot falls outside the chapter — ask the State Plumbing Board for a written answer.

</CalloutBox>

### Liability and Insurance

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="As owner-builder, the liability is yours">

As an owner-builder in Louisiana:

- You're personally liable for injuries on-site (workers' comp strongly recommended if you pay any labor)
- Builder's risk insurance is obtainable but priced higher than for licensed contractors — and in coastal parishes, wind and flood coverage are their own expensive conversation
- Some lenders require owner-builders to carry liability coverage during construction
- Louisiana's New Home Warranty Act and seller-disclosure rules create obligations that outlast the build

</CalloutBox>

### Seller Disclosure and the New Home Warranty Act

Louisiana's **New Home Warranty Act** (La. R.S. 9:3141 et seq.) sets statutory warranties a "builder" owes a buyer (one year on workmanship, two on systems, five on major structural). When you build for yourself and later sell, understand how this and Louisiana's residential property disclosure obligations apply to you — any known defects, unpermitted work, or code issues must be disclosed. Talk to a Louisiana real-estate attorney before selling an owner-built home.

### Foundation and Frost — A Non-Issue, But Soil Is Everything

Frost depth is essentially a non-factor in Louisiana — footings are shallow by northern standards. The foundation challenge here isn't frost; it's **soil**. In south Louisiana, soft, compressible delta soils and active subsidence drive the foundation design (often deep foundations / pilings). See the hazard section below — this is where Louisiana foundations get expensive.

## Hurricane, Flood, and Soft-Soil Hazards — The Defining Challenge

This is the section that matters most in Louisiana. The state's entire modern code exists because of these hazards, and they will shape your design, your budget, and your insurance more than anything else.

### Hurricane and High Wind

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="You are building for 130–160 mph winds in much of the state">

Coastal and south Louisiana are among the highest-wind design regions in the country. Engineer the structural load path — foundation to roof — to the LSUCC wind provisions, and don't value-engineer the connectors.

</CalloutBox>

Under the 2021 LSUCC, design wind speeds run roughly:

- **130–140 mph** in north and central parishes (East Baton Rouge, Lafayette, Caddo)
- **140–150 mph** on the north shore (St. Tammany) and inland south
- **150–160 mph** in the coastal parishes (Cameron, Plaquemines, St. Bernard, lower Jefferson, Lafourche, Terrebonne) and within a mile of the coast

What this means for your build:

- **Continuous load path**: Hurricane straps and hold-downs tying roof to walls to foundation, sized to the wind zone
- **Wind-borne debris protection**: Within ~1 mile of the coast where wind speeds exceed 140 mph, glazing must be impact-rated or protected (shutters) — a real cost item
- **Roof**: Sealed roof deck, ring-shank nailing patterns, and rated coverings; the LSUCC incorporates many **FORTIFIED**-style provisions, and going to the full IBHS FORTIFIED standard can earn insurance discounts
- **Gable ends and overhangs**: Braced and detailed to resist uplift

### Flood and Freeboard (Post-Katrina)

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Louisiana sets a freeboard floor by reference — and both claims in circulation are wrong">

You will read that Louisiana requires BFE plus one foot statewide, and you will read that Louisiana *removed* its statewide freeboard and left elevation entirely to parishes. **Neither matches the current text.** Louisiana writes no freeboard number into its code, but its amendment to 2021 IRC Section R322.1 does set a floor:

> "The local jurisdictions, utilizing flood plain managers, shall have the authority to adopt higher freeboard amounts as needed (CRS, etc.) **but shall not have the authority to adopt freeboard amounts less than those required in ASCE-24-14**."

So ASCE 24-14 is a statewide minimum applied by reference; your parish may go above it and **may not go below it**. The figure for your lot depends on your flood zone and flood design class. The Commission's approved 2024 IRC language carries the identical sentence forward, so this is policy, not an accident of one cycle.

</CalloutBox>

Flood is the hazard that reshaped Louisiana construction after Katrina:

- **Find your BFE**: Your FEMA flood zone and base flood elevation set the starting point for how high the lowest floor must be. A surveyor's elevation certificate documents it
- **Get the actual number in writing**: Ask your **parish floodplain manager** for the required lowest-floor elevation for your lot, and ask separately whether the parish has adopted freeboard **above** the ASCE 24-14 floor. Those are two different questions and you want both answers on paper
- **The statutory hook**: R.S. 37:3738(B)(3) provides that for residential and commercial construction, "the standards published by the Federal Emergency Management Agency for the National Flood Insurance Program apply"
- **Coastal A and V zones**: Near the coast, open foundations (pilings) with breakaway walls and flood-resistant materials below the design flood elevation are required
- **Citation note**: the two official printings head this amendment differently — one says Section R322.2.1, the other R322.1. Cite it as Louisiana's amendment to IRC Section R322, LAC 17:I.107, and quote the text rather than leaning on the sub-number

Elevation isn't just code compliance — every foot above BFE meaningfully cuts your flood-insurance cost for the life of the home. Many parishes and the NFIP Community Rating System push higher than the state floor for exactly that reason.

### Subsidence and Soft Soils

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="In south Louisiana, the ground is moving under you">

The Mississippi Delta has no stable bedrock near the surface, and the New Orleans metro subsides roughly 0.2 to 0.8+ inches per year. Conventional slabs crack and tilt. Below I-10/I-12, plan for a geotechnical investigation and likely a deep (pile) foundation.

</CalloutBox>

South Louisiana foundations are a different animal:

- **Soft, compressible soils**: Organic-rich delta soils settle under load; differential settlement causes the classic sticking doors, drywall cracks, and sloping floors
- **Subsidence**: Regional sinking (worsened by historic groundwater withdrawal) means even well-built slabs can move over time
- **Deep foundations**: In much of the New Orleans area and the coastal parishes, **timber, concrete, or auger-cast pilings** driven to bearing strata are standard for homes — this is the single biggest cost difference from building in north Louisiana or Ohio
- **Get borings first**: A geotechnical report ($1,500–$4,000) tells you whether you need pilings and how deep — never skip it in the southern parishes

North Louisiana (Shreveport, Monroe) sits on firmer ground and behaves more like a conventional slab market; the soil problem is concentrated in the south.

### Formosan Termites

<CalloutBox type="warning" title="Louisiana has the worst termite pressure in the country">

The invasive **Formosan subterranean termite** thrives in Louisiana's heat and humidity. Soil pretreatment under new construction is effectively required, and skipping it is a costly mistake.

</CalloutBox>

Termite protection is a standard, near-mandatory part of new construction here:

- **Soil pretreatment**: Treating the soil with an approved termiticide before the slab pour is the common method (regulated by the Louisiana Department of Agriculture and Forestry)
- **Detailing**: Keep wood and cellulose away from grade; maintain clearances and a visible inspection gap at the foundation
- **Cost**: $300–$900 for pretreatment — trivial against the damage Formosan colonies cause

## Special Louisiana Considerations

### Insurance Is Part of the Build Decision

In coastal Louisiana, **wind and flood insurance availability and cost** can rival the cost of the structure itself. Before you commit to a lot, price coverage. Building to FORTIFIED and elevating well above BFE are two of the most effective ways to control premiums — design for them from day one.

### Historic Districts (New Orleans)

If you're building or rebuilding in New Orleans, design review may run through the **Historic District Landmarks Commission (HDLC)** or **Vieux Carré Commission (VCC)** in addition to Safety & Permits. This adds review time and design constraints. Budget for it.

### Septic and Sewage (Rural Parishes)

The [Louisiana Department of Health](https://ldh.la.gov/) regulates individual sewage systems, and there are four things about the Louisiana system that catch owner-builders out.

**The permit is a state permit, issued through a parish office.** It comes from the **state health officer** by way of your parish health unit — a state office that happens to sit in your parish, not parish government. It issues in **two stages**: an approval to construct, then a final approval after inspection (LAC 51:XIII.701).

**You may install a conventional system yourself, but not a mechanical one.** LAC 51:XIII.705.A: an installer license "shall not be required … for an individual wishing to install an individual sewerage system, **other than an individual mechanical plant**, for his own private, personal use." The health department's own form is stricter than the rule reads — you must do the actual installation, and "hiring an unlicensed person to perform any part of the installation … will not be allowed under this exception." Nothing may be covered until an Office of Public Health sanitarian has inspected it.

**Louisiana has a statewide minimum lot size, and it is a lot-buying decision.** Under LAC 51:XIII.511.B.4, a home on an individual sewage system needs **22,500 sq ft with 125 ft of frontage**. Smaller lots are possible only in parishes that have "enacted and enforce a formal sewage permitting system," and two of those three reduced sizes **require a mechanical plant** — which, per the paragraph above, you are not allowed to install yourself. A small Louisiana lot quietly pushes you off the DIY path without anyone saying so out loud. Check the lot size before you buy, not after.

**High water tables in south Louisiana often rule out conventional absorption fields**, which is what drives so many south-Louisiana sites to the aerobic treatment units in the table below — and back into licensed-installer territory.

Draw the lot to scale before you decide where the house sits: the free [Site Plan Studio](/site-plan-studio) will show you whether the treatment unit, its field and the well all fit with the separations the parish is going to measure. One number worth knowing going in: the separation from a **private** water well to your absorption field is **50 feet**, not the 100 feet printed all over the internet — the table's own footnote reduces it for a private well, and LDH's applicant packet says the same thing independently.

### Wells

Wells are the mirror image of septic in Louisiana: you may dig your own drainfield, but **you may not drill your own well**. LAC 56:I.307.A requires that "all water wells, regardless of use or type" be drilled or constructed by a **licensed contractor (driller)**. There is no hand-auger loophole — the exemption people find at LAC 56:I.105.B.2 is an exemption from *registration*, not from the licensed-driller rule.

Three more corrections to what you'll read elsewhere:

- **The agency has a new name.** Well regulation sits with the **Louisiana Department of Conservation and Energy**, renamed from the Department of Energy and Natural Resources on **1 October 2025** — and DNR before that. It has moved four times; the rule text itself still names older agencies in places
- **There is no permit for a domestic well.** In a non-critical ground water area, a **notification is due within 60 days *after* installation** (LAC 43:VI.701.C), not before it. Registration is the **driller's** duty, not yours (LAC 56:I.105.A)
- **Levees are a real siting constraint here.** A well may not be drilled within **250 ft of the land-side toe of a levee**, and within **1,500 ft** of any state or federal flood-control levee you need the levee board's permission (LAC 56:I.317). Given how much of Louisiana sits behind a levee, check this before you pick the well location — it appears in no generic siting tool

## Common Questions

**Do I need a license to build my own house in Louisiana?** Not if it's your own personal residence. La. R.S. 37:2157(A)(13) exempts owners who supervise, superintend, oversee or direct construction of a home they'll live in — so you may act as your own general contractor and hire licensed subs. You file a notarized affidavit claiming exemption from licensure with the permit office before the permit issues. Without the exemption, a residential contractor license is required once project value reaches $50,000. (If you've seen R.S. 37:2170 cited for this, that section was repealed in 2022.)

**Can you build your own house without a permit in Louisiana?** No. Louisiana's LSUCC is mandatory statewide — every parish and municipality must enforce it under R.S. 37:3737(A)(1), which says in terms that "nothing in this Chapter allows any local government to avoid enforcement." Even lightly staffed rural parishes require permits and inspections. Every parish government in the state holds an active code-enforcement credential with the Commission. There are no "no-code" parishes.

**What is the Louisiana owner-builder exemption?** It's the statutory exemption at La. R.S. 37:2157(A)(13) that lets a property owner build their own personal residence without a contractor license — limited to one residence per year, with the year running from the date the certificate of occupancy issues, and documented by a notarized board-provided affidavit. It is written in management verbs, so it covers directing subcontractors, not just doing the work yourself.

**How much does a Louisiana owner-builder permit cost?** The permit itself is moderate — for a 2,000 sq ft home, on the order of $1,000 in East Baton Rouge and roughly $1,800 in New Orleans, with total permit-related cost usually in the $4,500–$9,500 range. Treat all of those as planning figures and confirm the current schedule with the office that will issue your permit. The bigger Louisiana costs are foundation (pilings in the south), wind hardening, flood elevation, and insurance.

**Which Louisiana parishes are best for owner-builders?** St. Tammany and Ascension offer the best mix of buildable ground and resale; East Baton Rouge has the most transparent fee schedule; Lafayette sits in a milder wind zone. Coastal parishes and Orleans Parish are the hardest (wind, flood, pilings, historic review).

## Final Thoughts for Louisiana Owner-Builders

Louisiana is a paradox for owner-builders. It has one of the strongest, most demanding building codes in the country — and a clear statutory right to build your own home anyway. You won't find the lax, unregulated freedom of rural Ohio or Texas here. What you get instead is a code that exists for a reason: to keep your house standing through 150 mph winds and your floor above the next flood.

The big decisions:

1. **Know your hazard trio**: wind zone, flood/BFE, and soil. These three — not the permit fee — determine your budget. Below I-10, assume pilings until a geotech report says otherwise.
2. **Build above the floor, not just to it**: Louisiana sets a statewide freeboard minimum by reference to ASCE 24-14 that your parish may exceed and may not undercut. Get the required lowest-floor elevation from your parish floodplain manager in writing — and then go higher anyway, because every foot over BFE pays you back in flood-insurance savings for the life of the home.
3. **Watch the calendar as hard as the budget**: The codes in effect on the day you *apply* are the codes your house is judged by (R.S. 37:3734), and the 2024 I-Codes and 2023 NEC land January 1, 2027. If you're close, price both editions before you decide when to file.
4. **Go FORTIFIED if you're near the coast**: The wind hardening the code already pushes you toward is worth completing for the insurance discount.
5. **Use the exemption correctly, and cite it correctly**: One residence per year measured from your CO date, your own home to live in, notarized affidavit before the permit issues — and cite R.S. 37:2157(A)(13), not the repealed 37:2170. Don't blur the line into spec-building.
6. **Price insurance before you buy the lot**: In coastal parishes, premiums can change the entire economics of the project. Check the lot size while you're at it — 22,500 sq ft is the statewide minimum for a home on an individual sewage system.

Louisiana rewards the owner-builder who respects the environment they're building in. The code is your ally against the storm — build to it generously, and you'll have a home that's not just permitted, but genuinely resilient.

      question: "Do I need to elevate my house in a Louisiana flood zone?",
      answer: "Yes, and the two claims in circulation about how much are both wrong. Louisiana does not write a number like 'BFE plus one foot' into its code, but it does not leave elevation purely to parishes either. Its amendment to 2021 IRC Section R322.1 provides that local jurisdictions 'shall have the authority to adopt higher freeboard amounts as needed (CRS, etc.) but shall not have the authority to adopt freeboard amounts less than those required in ASCE-24-14.' So ASCE 24-14 sets a statewide floor by reference, your parish may go above it and may not go below it, and the figure for your lot depends on your flood zone and flood design class. Ask your parish floodplain manager in writing for the required lowest-floor elevation, and ask separately whether the parish has adopted freeboard above the state floor. In coastal V and Coastal A zones, open (pile) foundations with breakaway walls and flood-resistant materials are required. Building higher than the minimum also lowers your flood-insurance premiums."

      question: "Can I install my own septic system or drill my own well in Louisiana?",
      answer: "Septic yes, well no. Three limits catch people: you must do the actual installation yourself, hiring an unlicensed person for any part of it voids the exception, nothing may be covered until an Office of Public Health sanitarian has inspected it, and a mechanical plant — the aerobic unit Louisiana leans on wherever soils and water tables defeat a conventional field — must be installed by a licensed installer.       question: "Why are foundations so expensive in south Louisiana?",
      answer: "Because the Mississippi Delta has no stable bedrock near the surface, the soils are soft and compressible, and the New Orleans metro is subsiding roughly 0.2 to 0.8+ inches per year. Conventional slabs crack and tilt over time, so homes in much of south Louisiana use deep foundations — timber, concrete, or auger-cast pilings driven to bearing strata. A geotechnical report ($1,500-$4,000) determines whether pilings are needed; the deep foundation itself can add $10,000-$40,000 or more versus a simple slab.
